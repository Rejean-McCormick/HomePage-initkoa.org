The Democratic Illusion: The People Vote, Money Governs

The people have the right to vote.

The Democratic Illusion: The People Vote, Money Governs

The people have the right to vote.

The rich have the means to influence that vote, capture public money, protect their fortunes, shape the rules of the game, and survive scandals.

That is the democratic illusion.

We are told that democracy means power to the people. But voting is not governing. Dropping a ballot into a box every four years does not give citizens control over the media, banks, major corporations, public contracts, subsidies, electoral rules, advertising campaigns, consulting firms, lobbying networks, or the machinery of influence.

The people choose, but they choose inside a framework prepared by others.

This illusion rests on several mechanisms: unequal influence before the vote, electoral rules that protect established parties, the capture of public money by private networks, the gap between the damage caused and the money recovered, and a form of material dependence that leaves citizens legally free but economically trapped.

The people possess the ritual of power.

The rich possess the means of power.

Voting Is Not Power

One person, one vote sounds fair. In principle, it is. The problem is not that every citizen has an equal vote. The problem is that citizens are not equal before they vote.

They are not equal in access to information, political education, time, stability, media literacy, economic security, or resistance to manipulation.

One person may spend years studying parties, public policy, economics, government lies, and the hidden interests behind political promises. Another may vote under the influence of a slogan, a manufactured fear, a targeted advertisement, a populist speech, an immediate personal benefit, or carefully cultivated resentment.

In the ballot box, both votes count the same.

But before the ballot box, everything is unequal.

The vote is equal. The manufacturing of opinion is not.

Those with money can buy visibility. They can finance campaigns. They can hire experts. They can produce advertisements. They can saturate social media. They can repeat a message until it feels like common sense. They can name enemies, amplify fears, simplify complex problems, and present their private interests as if they were the public interest.

Donald Trump is the extreme example of this logic: turning anger into loyalty, lies into identity, spectacle into power. But Trump is not an anomaly. He is a symptom. He reveals a weakness in modern democracy: the people vote, but those who control attention prepare the ground.

A manipulated people can appear to vote freely while choosing inside an imagination manufactured by others.

The Rules of the Game Protect Those Who Already Win

Even the voting system protects established parties.

Canada uses first-past-the-post. In each riding, the candidate who comes first wins, even without an absolute majority. This system does not directly reward a party’s share of the popular vote. It rewards the ability to come first locally.

That difference matters.

A party can receive real support across the country — 5%, 10%, even 15% of the vote — and still remain nearly absent from Parliament if that support is dispersed. Meanwhile, a major party can win a majority of seats with far less than a majority of votes.

The 2015 federal election showed this clearly. The Liberals received 39.5% of the vote, but about 54.4% of the seats. The NDP received 19.7% of the vote, but about 13.0% of the seats. The Greens received 3.4% of the vote, but about 0.3% of the seats.

That is the distortion: some votes become power; others nearly disappear.

A proportional reform would not merely have “possibly” helped emerging parties. It would have done so mechanically. If seats more closely reflect votes, then votes for smaller parties stop being wasted. They become seats. They become presence. They become leverage.

A proportional system would have reduced false majorities. It would have forced major parties to negotiate. It would have created space for emerging parties, third parties, and political movements that the current system suffocates before they can matter.

That is precisely why it threatened established parties.

Justin Trudeau promised that the 2015 election would be the last under first-past-the-post. But once in power, the party that had promised to change the rules had become the beneficiary of those rules. The reform was abandoned.

Before gaining power, a party can denounce the system.

After winning because of that system, it has every reason to preserve it.

That is the perfect democratic conflict of interest: the winners of the game decide whether the rules of the game should change.

No one should be surprised when the rules remain the same.

The Real Power Is Economic

The ordinary citizen has a vote.

The rich person has one too.

But the rich person has much more than a vote.

He has capital. Lawyers. Accounting firms. Lobbyists. Companies. Donors. Contacts. Access to decision-makers. The ability to threaten relocation. The ability to demand subsidies in the name of jobs. The ability to influence tax law. The ability to hire public relations firms. The ability to sue. The ability to wait. The ability to defend himself for years.

The citizen has a voice.

The rich have an amplification system.

Democratic equality exists on paper. It exists in the ritual. It exists on election day. But it collapses when we look at the real means different people have to shape society.

The people vote.

Money organizes the ground on which the people vote.

The People Are Forced to Pay

Citizens must pay taxes.

In principle, that is normal. A society needs roads, hospitals, schools, transportation, courts, inspectors, care, and infrastructure. The problem is not that the state collects money to fund the common good.

The problem begins when that money becomes a source of private enrichment.

The people are forced to pay. They pay taxes. They pay cost overruns. They pay mistakes. They pay consultants. They pay inflated contracts. They pay failed projects. They pay prestige travel. They pay subsidies to large corporations. They pay bailouts. They pay losses.

Meanwhile, the best-positioned networks know where to plug themselves in to extract public money.

Sometimes it is criminal theft: fraud, corruption, kickbacks, false invoices.

But often it is cleaner than that. A non-competitive contract. A strategic subsidy. An outside consulting firm. A poorly managed digital project. Innovation funding. A diplomatic summit. A public-health emergency. An administrative failure.

The words change.

The bill remains with the people.

What Never Comes Back

Public scandals are often told as stories of crime and punishment. Someone is accused. Someone pleads guilty. Someone receives a sentence. Some money is seized. A fraction is repaid. The file is then placed in collective memory as if justice has done its job.

But that way of telling the story is misleading.

The real scandal is not only that someone stole, defrauded, overbilled, captured, or wasted public money. The real scandal is the gap between what the people lose and what actually returns.

Some scandals are crimes. Others are mismanagement, legal capture, failed industrial bets, or public money exposed to private risk. These are not the same legal categories. But politically, the pattern is similar: money leaves the public, some of it may come back, and a large part remains lost, dispersed, transformed, or impossible to recover.

The Sponsorship scandal is a clear example. The program cost about $250 million. More than $100 million went to agencies in fees and commissions. About $8.09 million was recovered as improperly received funds. If we compare that recovery only to the agency fees and commissions, more than $90 million did not return. If we compare it to the full program, the gap is more than $240 million.

And even that does not count the rest: the inquiry, the trials, the lawyers, the years of institutional work, the loss of trust, the political cynicism, the democratic damage.

That is what never gets repaid.

The MUHC scandal is even more disturbing because it involved a hospital. The amounts discussed included about $22.5 million in payments linked to the hospital contract, with a $10 million bribe acknowledged in Yanaï Elbaz’s case. Roughly $6 million was frozen, seized, or surrendered. Even on direct payments alone, that leaves about $16.5 million outside recovery. But the deeper damage is not only financial. A hospital contract was contaminated. Honest competitors were pushed aside. Investigations and proceedings consumed public resources. Trust in the health system was damaged.

Recovering a few million does not repay a hospital corrupted by private enrichment.

In the Sami Bebawi case, the gap is brutal. A fine of about $24.5 million was imposed. About $100,000 was reportedly repaid. On paper, justice names a multi-million-dollar amount. In reality, the public sees almost nothing return.

A court order is not a repayment.

A conviction is not a repair.

A prison sentence is not the return of public money.

In Laval, the City recovered about $60 million after corruption and collusion cases. That sounds significant, and it is. But even there, the recovery cannot be confused with full repair. You cannot repay years of contaminated administration. You cannot repay every inflated contract. You cannot repay honest businesses pushed aside. You cannot repay citizens’ trust after they understand that their city was treated as a machine for enriching networks.

Gilles Vaillancourt returned about $8.5 to $8.6 million. Yet after prison and repayment, he was not reduced to the condition of the citizens who had paid the bill. Publicly reported details still showed a family environment associated with important assets: a Florida condo valued around $900,000, a Laval condo reportedly paid in cash by his spouse, money recovered after seizure, and even the capacity to pursue the City.

The people recovered a proven portion.

They did not recover the Vaillancourt system.

Tony Accurso illustrates the same problem differently. The point is not only that he was convicted. The point is that a man connected to rigged public contracts could still be associated with major assets after prison. A private estate sold for more than $23 million after conviction shows that the judicial sanction did not destroy the economic power surrounding him.

Jean Lafleur shows another kind of gap. The federal government claimed nearly $6 million. About $246,000 was recovered. What the state claims, what it proves, what a court orders, and what actually comes back are four different realities.

Then there are the cases where no one needs to prove criminal theft to see the public loss.

SAAQclic was budgeted at about $638 million and rose to at least $1.1 billion. That is roughly $500 million in overrun. Who repays that? No one. The citizen pays for the project, the failure, the corrections, and the consequences.

Phoenix was supposed to modernize federal payroll. It instead produced years of failure. Its replacement, Dayforce, is estimated at more than $4.2 billion. Citizens paid for the failed system, then pay again to escape the failed system.

ArriveCAN was estimated at $59.5 million, while the exact cost was difficult to determine because of poor financial records. When the state cannot clearly reconstruct the bill, how can the people control their money?

McKinsey represents another form of extraction. About $209 million in federal contracts were examined, with roughly $200 million spent. This is not presented as simple fraud. It is worse in another way: the state pays a private firm to think in its place. The money leaves. It becomes fees, reports, presentations, recommendations, dependency. The people pay, and the state weakens its own internal capacity.

Bombardier reveals the logic of legal capture. Quebec invested US$1 billion in the C Series. Ottawa announced $372.5 million in repayable contributions. Meanwhile, six senior executives received US$32.6 million in compensation for 2016, about 50% more than the previous year.

This was not criminal theft.

It was cleaner than that.

That is precisely the problem.

Public money enters a company in the name of jobs, innovation, and industrial strategy. Executives keep receiving enormous compensation. Even when aid is technically structured as an investment or repayable contribution, the political damage remains: the people carry the risk while the top keeps its privileges.

Lion Electric follows the same logic. Governments announced nearly $100 million to support a battery plant. A few years later, the company showed liabilities of about US$499.8 million and entered creditor protection. At the same time, senior executives had compensation above the million-dollar level.

We cannot say every salary dollar came directly from government money. Money is not marked that way.

But that is exactly the problem. When public money enters the financial ecosystem of an unprofitable company, it supports the structure. It prolongs the project. It reassures creditors. It allows the organization to continue. And while it continues, executives are paid.

If the project succeeds, they were visionaries.

If it fails, the people helped pay the bill.

Northvolt was sold as a major green industrial bet. Quebec lost about $270 million in the parent company. A separate loan may be partly recovered, but that does not repay the capital loss. Recovering one pocket of money does not erase the loss in another.

Medicago is simpler. Ottawa advanced $173 million. After the company shut down, $40 million was recovered, along with some assets. That leaves about $133 million not recovered in direct cash. Officials can call it a partial recovery. But for the public, the arithmetic is clear: $173 million went out; $40 million came back.

The rest did not.

And then there are prestige expenses: Trudeau’s India trip, with a bill exceeding $1.5 million; the Aga Khan trip, which led to conflict-of-interest findings; Governor General Mary Simon’s Middle East trip, around $1.3 million; the Summit of the Americas in Quebec City, about $156 million; and the 2010 G8 and G20 summits, with $1.1 billion in approved funding and projected costs of $664 million.

Hotels, aircraft, catering, fences, security zones, delegations, ceremonies, photographs.

Everything is justified by protocol.

Everything is paid by the people.

And every time, we are told it is an exception.

A mistake.

Mismanagement.

An emergency.

Complexity.

An isolated case.

A lesson learned.

But at some point, exceptions begin to look like a rule.

Illegal Theft and Legal Siphoning

Two mechanisms must be distinguished.

The first is illegal theft: fraud, corruption, false invoices, kickbacks, collusion, breach of trust. That one sometimes ends up in court. A culprit can be named. A sentence can be imposed. A partial repayment can be ordered.

The second is cleaner, more administrative, and harder to attack: legal siphoning.

In these cases, no one has necessarily committed a crime. The money leaves legally. It passes through programs, subsidies, loans, tax credits, repayable contributions, strategic investments, consulting contracts, guarantees, innovation funds, and industrial policies.

But the political result can be almost the same: public money leaves the people, enters private structures, enriches executives, consultants, suppliers, shareholders, creditors, or business networks, and then does not fully return when the project fails.

That is where words matter.

When money is taken illegally, it is called theft.

When money is legally absorbed by the powerful, it is called economic development.

When a small person receives too much money from the state, it is called fraud.

When a large corporation receives hundreds of millions and fails, it is called an industrial bet.

When a poor person does not repay, they must pay.

When a large company fails to deliver its promises, we add nuance: difficult market, global context, transition, restructuring, unforeseen circumstances, complexity.

In fraud, the people are robbed.

In legal siphoning, the people are convinced that paying for the rich is in their interest.

The Harm Caused Exceeds the Punishment Received

When we talk about corruption, fraud, or the capture of public money, the focus is often placed on the conviction: this person pleaded guilty, that one received four years in prison, another repaid a few million, another received a fine.

But that is not the heart of the scandal.

The heart of the scandal is the gap between the damage caused and the punishment received.

A man can serve three years in prison. But if his system diverted millions, enriched his circle, distorted contracts, destroyed public trust, cost years of investigations, and mobilized prosecutors, judges, police, commissions, auditors, and lawyers, then the sentence does not repair the damage. It closes a file. It does not repay society.

What matters is not that a culprit returned part of the money.

What matters is everything he did not return.

He did not return the years of distorted contracts.

He did not return the honest competitors pushed aside.

He did not return the indirect profits.

He did not return the fortunes built around the system.

He did not return the enriched relatives.

He did not return the cost of the investigation.

He did not return the legal fees.

He did not return the institutions’ time.

He did not return public trust.

He did not return the services that could have been funded with that money.

He did not return the damaged democracy.

Prison does not repay distorted contracts.

Partial repayment does not repay years of corruption.

A fine does not repay destroyed trust.

A conviction does not repay an ecosystem of enrichment.

The real scandal is not that rich people steal and are sometimes convicted.

The real scandal is that they can cause social harm, receive an individual punishment, and leave the people with the rest of the bill.

Prison as a Cost of Doing Business

To understand the weakness of some economic sanctions, we sometimes have to do the cold calculation.

If millions remain out of reach after a conviction, prison does not reset the books. It punishes a person, but it does not necessarily recover the money, empty the enriched networks, or repair the public harm.

In the Bebawi case, if the fine was about $24.5 million and only about $100,000 was repaid, the unrecovered gap is about $24.4 million. Over a 10-year prison term, that is about $2.44 million per year in unrecovered money.

In the Elbaz-MUHC case, if one uses the broader $22.5 million scheme and subtracts the roughly $6 million frozen, seized, or surrendered, the unrecovered gap is about $16.5 million. Over 39 months, or about 3.25 years, that equals roughly $5.1 million per year.

In Jean Lafleur’s case, using the cautious calculation, an acknowledged fraud of about $1.57 million minus roughly $246,000 recovered leaves about $1.32 million unrecovered. Over 42 months, or about 3.5 years, that is about $380,000 per year. If we compare the federal claim of nearly $6 million with the same recovery, the unrecovered gap is about $5.75 million, or about $1.64 million per year.

These numbers are not salaries. They do not claim to capture the entire legal reality. They show only one thing: when millions remain unrecovered despite prison, the sentence becomes economically ambiguous.

One can say a man received 39 months, 42 months, or 10 years. But if, after that sentence, millions remain out of reach, society has not been repaired.

Prison confines an individual. It does not automatically recover the money. It does not empty relatives’ accounts. It does not undo purchased properties, fed businesses, enriched partners, favoured children, protected spouses, or grateful networks.

They say the money disappeared.

But the money did not disappear.

It went somewhere.

It became houses, investments, contracts, companies, gifts, protections, lifestyle, influence.

When the unrecovered part of a crime exceeds what an honest worker could earn in several lifetimes, the punishment is not complete justice.

It is a discount.

Repayment Does Not Repay the System

Even when there is repayment, we should not be impressed too quickly.

Repayment usually concerns what has been proven, documented, prosecuted, and recognized in one specific case. It does not concern everything that was never discovered. It does not concern all the years during which the same method may have worked without drawing attention. It does not concern the entire fortune accumulated around the same networks, access, contracts, and questionable practices.

It is like someone stealing ten times from a store, getting caught once, and saying: “Fine, I’ll return the item.”

No.

That is not repair.

It is only the return of the visible piece of the theft.

The store remains the loser for the other nine times. And if the thief built his lifestyle through repeated theft, returning one object does not change the fact that his wealth partly comes from the system he exploited.

The same is true with public fraud.

When a powerful person is caught, we look at a specific file: one contract, one period, one amount, one set of evidence. But his fortune does not necessarily come from one file. It may come from a long history of privileged access, overbilling, steered contracts, political relationships, nominees, related companies, commissions, favours returned, and opportunities ordinary citizens never had.

Repayment therefore does not prove that justice has been done.

It only proves that one piece of the theft became visible enough to be claimed.

The real question is not: did he repay what we managed to prove?

The real question is: how much did he keep from everything we never managed to prove?

And that is where the people always lose.

Fortune as a Tool of Power

When someone uses a gun to commit a crime, the gun can be taken away.

When someone uses a professional licence to commit fraud, the licence can be revoked.

When someone uses a company as a criminal instrument, some assets tied to that company can sometimes be seized.

But when a powerful person uses his fortune, companies, relationships, lawyers, nominees, and influence to divert public money, the entire tool of power is almost never taken away.

What is proven is seized.

What is traceable is claimed.

What is still available is confiscated.

But the rest often remains around him: companies, relatives, trusts, properties, investments, contracts, reputation, network, social access.

That is where justice becomes insufficient.

Because fortune is not only a reward. It is a tool. It is a means of influence. It is protection. It is the ability to start again. It is a shield against collapse.

If someone uses his fortune and influence to defraud, corrupt, or divert public money, why does he then keep the structure of power that allowed him to do it?

Why is that fortune treated as neutral property when it may have been built, protected, or enlarged by the very abuses we claim to punish?

Justice punishes the act.

But it often leaves standing the social instrument that made the act possible.

Michael Milken pleaded guilty to securities-related offences, served less than two years after a reduced sentence, paid massive penalties, and was barred from the securities industry. Yet after his sentence, his fortune was still estimated in the billions. A historic financial conviction did not remove him from the global economic elite.

Martha Stewart served five months in prison in the ImClone case, yet her fortune remained in the hundreds of millions. Her conviction became a parenthesis in a career of wealth.

Conrad Black served prison time after fraud and obstruction convictions in the United States, though some charges were later overturned. His wealth was reduced, but his life remained tied to high-value real estate, social capital, and elite networks.

Ronald Weinberg, convicted in the Cinar fraud, illustrates another mechanism: money can be extracted, transformed into wealth, and moved before justice fully arrives. The sale of shares reportedly brought about US$18 million to Weinberg and Micheline Charest before the criminal aftermath.

In Quebec, Tony Accurso’s post-conviction estate sale above $23 million and Vaillancourt’s still-comfortable family asset environment show the same deeper problem: punishment does not necessarily destroy economic power.

The public knows about a conviction, a bankruptcy, a judgment, a seizure, a partial repayment. But it does not always know about transferred properties, family assets, remaining companies, trusts, enriched relatives, protected partners, and networks that continue to exist.

Justice recovers what is proven, traceable, and still available.

It does not necessarily recover the economic power built around the crime.

The poor person who falls loses everything.

The rich person who falls falls into his network.

Justice Arrives After the Transfer of Wealth

Even when the powerful are caught, justice never truly repairs the damage.

It can convict. It can seize part of the money. It can order restitution in some cases. But it almost never puts the people back in the position they would have occupied if the theft, fraud, corruption, or abusive capture had not happened.

Because the real damage is not only accounting.

There is the amount defrauded. There are years of possible but unproven abuse. There are distorted contracts. There are honest competitors pushed aside. There are weakened public services. There are investigation costs. There are police officers. There are prosecutors. There are courts. There are commissions. There are reports. There are auditors. There are legal fees. There is institutional time swallowed up.

The people are robbed once by the fraud.

Then they pay a second time to try to prove they were robbed.

Then they pay again to prosecute, judge, negotiate, recover a fraction, produce reports, revise rules, and repair the damage.

And at the end, we call that justice.

But it is not repair.

It is cleanup after the money has already circulated.

Stolen money does not always remain in an account under the culprit’s name. It becomes a house. A company. A trust. An investment. A salary paid to a relative. A favour. A contract. A lifestyle. Paid tuition. A career launched. Property held in someone else’s name. A business relationship. A purchased reputation. Family security.

When justice arrives, the money is no longer simply “the money from the crime.”

It has become an environment of protection.

The culprit can lose a trial. He can serve a few years. He can repay part of the money. But around him, the world he enriched often remains standing.

The brother-in-law benefited.

The cousin benefited.

The business friend benefited.

The spouse benefited.

The children benefited.

The related companies benefited.

The network benefited.

Prison does not empty that network.

Partial repayment does not destroy that social wealth.

Justice can recover a sum. It never recovers the enrichment system built around that sum.

For small people, stealing from the state is a crime.

For the powerful, it is sometimes a bet: if you win, you keep everything; if you lose, you return part of it, serve some time, your network remains rich, and the people keep paying to clean up the mess.

Private Profits, Public Losses

This is the most obscene logic in the system.

When things go well, profits are private. Executives cash in. Shareholders cash in. Consultants cash in. Firms cash in. Companies speak of merit, vision, and entrepreneurial risk.

But when things go badly, the discourse changes.

The company becomes too important to fail. The industry becomes strategic. Jobs become threatened. The government must intervene. The taxpayer must help. The state must invest. The public must take the risk.

The small shop owner who fails loses his savings.

The large company that fails can sometimes get a meeting with a minister.

The ordinary citizen must live with his mistakes.

The powerful negotiate theirs.

This is not pure capitalism. It is assisted capitalism for the powerful and brutal individual responsibility for everyone else.

When the poor fail, it is their fault.

When the rich fail, it is a strategic issue.

Modern Economic Servitude

We must not confuse the present situation with historical slavery. Historical slavery was based on the direct ownership of human beings, legal violence, the denial of personhood, and the absence of fundamental rights. It is not the same thing.

But that does not mean modern freedom is complete.

Today, the citizen is legally free. He can choose his job. He can vote. He can speak. He can consume. He can move. He can criticize.

But he must pay to live.

He must pay rent, groceries, electricity, transportation, insurance, medication, debts, interest, education, clothing, communications. He must sell his time to survive. He is free to choose his employer, but he is not free not to work. He is free to vote, but often too exhausted to become informed. He is free to protest, but he risks his stability.

The modern chain is not on the foot.

It is in the bank account.

A people exhausted by survival does not have time to govern. It only has time to work, pay, sleep, and start again.

And that people is called sovereign.

Economic Concentration Destroys Local Power

Democracy should not be only political. It should also be economic.

A truly free people should be able to produce locally, repair locally, buy locally, trade locally, create locally. It should be able to sustain butcher shops, cobblers, carpenters, florists, bakeries, neighbourhood grocery stores, repair shops, and independent businesses.

But the current economy pushes in the opposite direction.

It concentrates. It absorbs. It franchises. It standardizes. It replaces local businesses with chains, artisans with platforms, repair workers with disposability, independent owners with branches.

We buy $100 shoes that last two seasons because it is the easy choice. Then we throw them away. Then we start again.

A $200 pair of shoes, durable, repairable, maintained by a cobbler, would be better for society. Less waste. Less transport. Less exploitation. More local trades. More know-how. More durability.

But the system often makes the bad choice cheaper than the good one.

What the citizen saves at the cash register, he later pays in pollution, dependence, lost trades, and the destruction of local life.

Here again, democracy recedes. Because a people that no longer owns its businesses, tools, trades, meeting places, and local economy becomes a people of dependent consumers.

It still votes.

But it depends on chains it does not control.

The People Are Not Sovereign; They Are Managed

This is the heart of the problem.

We are told the people are sovereign. In reality, they are mostly managed.

Managed by electoral campaigns.

Managed by banks.

Managed by rents.

Managed by employers.

Managed by debt.

Managed by platforms.

Managed by commercial chains.

Managed by media.

Managed by communications firms.

Managed by consultants.

Managed by tax rules.

Managed by private interests that know better than they do how to speak to power.

The people are asked for their opinion at election time. Then they are asked to go back to work while the powerful take care of the rest.

That is the democratic illusion.

It is not a dictatorship. The people have rights. They can vote. They can speak. They can demonstrate. They can criticize. Those rights are real and must be defended.

But they are not enough.

Because a democracy where the people vote, but where money influences opinion, funds campaigns, captures contracts, obtains subsidies, dictates economic priorities, destroys local power, and escapes the real consequences of its abuses, is not a complete democracy.

It is a façade democracy.

The people possess the ritual of power.

The rich possess the means of power.

We are given a ballot to convince us that we govern, while the real levers — money, contracts, media, corporations, economic rules, consultants, networks, and access to the state — remain in the hands of those who already possess them.

This is not power to the people.

It is the periodic consent of the people to a system that taxes them, exhausts them, uses them, and then asks them to call it freedom.
