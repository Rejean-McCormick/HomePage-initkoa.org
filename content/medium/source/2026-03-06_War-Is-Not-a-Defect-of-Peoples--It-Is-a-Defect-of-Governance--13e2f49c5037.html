War Is Not a Defect of Peoples. It Is a Defect of Governance.

When war intensifies, language deteriorates quickly. We stop speaking about governments, chains of command, interests, doctrines, budgets, propaganda. Instead we begin speaking in blocks: the Russians, the Ukrainians, the Iranians, the Americans — as if entire peoples thought with a single mind.

I refuse that shift.

Not merely because it is inaccurate, but because it prepares the ground for hatred. Once a people is treated as a morally unified entity, it becomes easier to despise, humiliate, and eventually justify doing the unacceptable to them. Wars are decided by structures of power — governments, command systems, institutions — and very often by structures that do not truly answer to the peoples in whose name they act.

The first requirement, if one wants to think about peace without naïveté, is therefore this: distinguish between peoples and governments. Without that distinction, we do not produce clarity — we produce collective resentment.

The Real Problem: Governance

In my view, contemporary wars are not primarily problems of peoples. They are problems of governance.

This means several things at once. It means the failure of political structures to address conflicts before they become military. It means the failure of information systems that cannot make options visible. It means the failure of public mechanisms that know how to finance war more easily than they know how to organize a way out.

In that sense, war is not only a tragedy. It is also an institutional confession of incapacity.

Corruption is often invoked. It exists. But corruption is not the central issue. The deeper problem is more pervasive and more ordinary: systemic incompetence, fragmented information, the inability to assemble relevant expertise and convert it into decisions that can actually be executed. When that capacity is absent, military force becomes the default instrument — because it is already structured, budgeted, commanded, and available.

Refuse Military Escalation, Demand Diplomatic Escalation

I am not advocating inaction. I am advocating a different form of power.

Military escalation is brutal, easy to trigger, and extremely difficult to control afterward. Diplomatic escalation requires something else: mediation, guarantees, verification mechanisms, negotiation channels, humanitarian corridors, partial compromises when total agreement is impossible.

It requires more skill than crushing an opponent with brute force. Precisely for that reason, it is the superior form of escalation.

Non-interventionism, as I understand it, does not mean moral withdrawal. It means not adding military power to an ongoing conflict, while continuously strengthening the diplomatic capacity to end it.

The “Defensive Only” Narrative

At this point, we must examine an argument that has become almost automatic.

When governments finance or arm one side in a war, they often say the assistance is strictly defensive. Presented that way, the action appears limited, almost clean. But without context, this phrase is a narrative rather than an analysis.

In war, money is fungible. Stockpiles are fungible. Capabilities are fungible. If external resources cover functions labeled defensive, internal resources that would have covered those functions can be redirected elsewhere. At the level of the system, the result is straightforward: the overall capacity to continue the war increases.

This observation does not deny aggression, suffering, or the need to protect civilians. It simply insists that societies examine structures rather than rely on reassuring labels.

War Debt: Not Only Reconstruction

War debt is often discussed as a future burden linked to reconstruction. But debt is not only contracted after destruction. It is also contracted during the war itself, to make the war sustainable.

This is visible in Ukraine. Figures relayed from Ukraine’s Ministry of Finance indicate that between 2022 and 2024 the country received about $98.04 billion in external financing. After subtracting the portion provided as grants, this corresponds to roughly $65.65 billion in loans over that period.

These loans are not separate from the war effort. The Ukrainian Ministry of Finance has explained that external financing covers social and humanitarian expenditures while domestic resources are directed toward security and defense. In other words, these loans help sustain the war budget.

Domestic borrowing adds to this. In 2024, Ukraine also raised approximately $15.7 billion through domestic government bonds to finance its budget.

The implication must be stated clearly. War does not only destroy the present. It mortgages the future. And when those debts carry interest, those who collect the interest draw a financial stream from a people already damaged by war. Physical destruction is followed by long-term financial constraint. It is a form of delayed economic subordination.

Ukraine as a Lesson in Cost

Ukraine illustrates with painful clarity what modern war produces: not only casualties and ruined cities, but massive reconstruction costs, public debt, financial dependence, political trauma, and social fragmentation.

The longer a war lasts, the more societies harden internally. Nuance becomes suspect. Compassion toward the other people is framed as weakness. Narratives close in on themselves, and populations become trapped between the decisions of their governments, the interests of external powers, and the self-perpetuating logic of war.

For this reason, the central question is not merely who is guilty. The deeper question is this: what made war easier than the exit from war? Until that question is addressed, we are treating symptoms rather than causes.

Canada Could Choose Another Role

Canada is not just another ally in the system. It sits geographically between the United States and Russia. That position does not automatically determine policy, but it opens a possibility: mediation rather than additional military alignment.

This possibility is not theoretical. In 2003, Prime Minister Jean Chrétien refused to support the United States in the invasion of Iraq, a war justified at the time largely by claims about weapons of mass destruction. In the House of Commons, he indicated that Canada would not participate without a new resolution from the United Nations Security Council.

The point is not to romanticize that moment. The point is that a Canadian government once refused participation in a war presented as necessary by Washington. A precedent therefore exists.

In the spirit of the argument advanced here, Canada could have pursued a stronger mediating role in the Ukraine conflict — not because aggression does not exist, but because adding military resources is not the same as building a path out of war. A country situated between major powers could have chosen to strengthen diplomacy rather than armament.

What Must Be Built

Peace does not arrive through good intentions alone. It requires capacity.

It requires the capacity to distinguish peoples from governments.
It requires the capacity to resist simplistic narratives.
It requires the capacity to fund diplomacy with the same seriousness as war.
It requires the capacity to make non-military options visible and operational.
It requires, finally, the capacity to prevent peoples already devastated by war from becoming long-term debtors of that war.

This is where governance returns to the center of the problem. A healthy society is not defined only by its values. It is defined by its ability to make non-military outcomes practical before destruction becomes the only visible path.

Conclusion

I am not asking anyone to deny violence. I am asking that we stop adding to violence the intellectual and moral mistake of confusing peoples with governments.

War is not a defect of peoples. It is a defect of governance.

And as long as we describe as “defensive assistance” what increases the system’s overall capacity for war, as long as we accept that war debt can bind generations already wounded by conflict, and as long as diplomatic escalation is treated as optional rather than strategic necessity, we will continue to prolong what we claim to oppose.

Peace requires more than indignation. It requires structure, competence, and political courage.
