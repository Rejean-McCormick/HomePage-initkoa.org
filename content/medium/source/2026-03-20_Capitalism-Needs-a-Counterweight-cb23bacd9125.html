Capitalism Needs a Counterweight

I am not fundamentally opposed to capitalism. I understand why it appeals to people, why it attracts them, and why so many still defend it. It can stimulate innovation. It can reward effort. It can push certain people to undertake, to create, to build. For some, it even becomes a kind of game: the challenge of seeing who can generate the most value, grow the biggest project, accumulate the most resources. In many cases, money then becomes an indicator of performance.

And one more thing must be acknowledged: when immense resources are concentrated in the hands of one person or one organization, it sometimes becomes possible to accomplish extraordinary things. Some great fortunes have made major technological advances possible, funded ambitious investments, and at times even enabled large-scale philanthropy. So it would be simplistic to say that all wealth is bad in itself.

But recognizing that does not mean ignoring capitalism’s distortions. The problem is not only what capitalism makes possible. The problem is also what it destroys along the way, what it demands of ordinary people, and the way wealth is so often accumulated.

Where I oppose capitalism as it exists today is when a fortune is built on the exploitation of the working class. It is when immense wealth is not the result of exceptional talent placed in service of the common good, but of a system that takes a great deal from those who have little, that squeezes workers to the limit, that turns every human need into a source of profit. And unfortunately, I have the impression that this is how most modern fortunes are built: not only through genius, but through extraction.

I do not want to target any one person in particular. This is not a matter of blaming a few individuals as though they alone were responsible for an entire system. My discomfort is broader than that. In general, when someone accumulates a gigantic fortune, it becomes hard not to wonder what could have been redistributed sooner. How many wages could have been higher? How many working conditions could have been more humane? How many communities could have been better supported? How many employees helped create that wealth without ever receiving a truly fair share of the result of their labor?

The problem, then, is not simply the existence of wealth. The problem is the excessive concentration of wealth while those who actually produce value live with fatigue, insecurity, or dependence.

Capitalism is often described as a system of freedom. In theory, anyone can start a business, create a shop, build an activity, become independent. But in reality, that freedom is deeply unequal. Small business owners, bakers, farmers, restaurateurs, artisans, manual workers, all those who simply want to build something useful and concrete, end up crushed under enormous pressure. To keep a small business alive, it is not enough to be competent. You have to be exceptionally resilient. You have to be willing to make disproportionate sacrifices. You have to accept an enormous mental burden. You often have to sacrifice your health, your family, your stability, and sometimes your dignity.

And even then, you still need luck.

That is not a healthy model. A society should not require a person to destroy their balance in life just to keep a bakery, a small farm, a workshop, or a small restaurant running. A healthy society should allow useful trades to survive without demanding permanent heroism.

What makes this situation even more absurd is that exploitation does not always appear in spectacular form. Sometimes it comes in the form of small fees, small percentages, small obligations, small intermediaries, all of them taking their share. Thousands of dollars here, hundreds there, and then twenty-five cents somewhere else: that is how an entire chain of extraction gets built.

Take the example of buying property. In theory, it is simply a transfer of ownership from one person to another, recorded in a registry. Yet that act quickly becomes surrounded by a whole series of costs we almost end up accepting as natural: notary fees, appraisal fees, surveying fees, various verifications, bank fees, administrative fees, interest, mandatory insurance, and so on. Of course, some of these services have a real purpose. Of course, a registry must exist, calculations must be made, protections must be in place. But are all these costs reasonable? Do they truly correspond to the work being done? Or have they also become compulsory checkpoints where more and more is always extracted from people?

Banks embody this logic especially well. They provide an essential service; no one can deny that. We need payment systems, credit, and stable financial infrastructure. It is normal for a service to have a cost. What is troubling is the constant slide from the legitimate coverage of a cost to the pursuit of extraordinary profit. When an indispensable service becomes a mechanism of permanent dependence, when it allows a few institutions to extract enormous value simply because they control a mandatory passage point, there is a form of modern exploitation there — often more polished, more legal, but no less real.

So what do we do with this?

I believe we are ready for a new model. Not necessarily to abolish capitalism entirely, nor to return to a pure form of communism, which failed when it tried to suffocate individual initiative. What I propose instead is a two-track model, a hybrid system in which capitalism and a structured logic of solidarity would coexist in parallel.

On one side, there could continue to be space for innovation, for large ambitions, for risky projects, for private enterprise and wealth creation. But on the other side, we would need to build a second economic pillar, a protected space in which essential small trades could exist without being subjected to the brutal logic of accumulation.

In this model, small businesses that are useful to society — bakeries, farms, workshops, kitchens, artisan shops, small manufacturers, places of local production — would not be left entirely to the law of the market. They would be supported, at least in part, by the collective system. Not in order to turn them into unproductive structures, and not in order to transform them into bureaucracies, but to give them the breathing room they need to sustain themselves, to pass on knowledge, and to serve.

At the heart of this model is education.

I believe education should happen much more through internships and apprenticeships. Public money already exists for training and education. That money could be redirected, at least in part, toward real workplaces that would also become places of transmission. A bakery could train people. A farm could train people. A restaurant could train people. A workshop could train people. Instead of completely separating school from work, we could create structures where learning happens in the field, in practice, in contact with reality.

In such a context, maximum performance would not be the primary objective. There would still be wealth creation, because the trade genuinely produces something: bread, vegetables, meals, furniture, objects, services. But that wealth would not serve first and foremost to enrich an owner or a boss who keeps accumulating more and more. It would serve to sustain the activity, to train people, to stabilize the workplace, to meet concrete needs, to nourish the community, and to keep know-how alive.

In other words, a business can create wealth without making private accumulation its primary objective.

That is the fundamental point.

In this model, the small entrepreneur would no longer be an isolated individual forced to carry everything alone, competing with better-financed giants and surrounded by an entire system of fixed costs. They would be part of a protected structure, where economic activity exists, but where its capacity to become a mere tool of exploitation is limited. The exact mechanism still needs to be defined, but it could take the form of public ownership, or collective ownership, just as vocational training centers and educational institutions already do. The important thing is not to lock in every administrative detail immediately. The important thing is to understand the logic: to remove certain essential trades from the constant pressure of capitalist accumulation.

This would not be a total rejection of the market. It would be a recognition that some sectors of economic life have too much social value to be abandoned entirely to profitability alone. Feeding, repairing, building, caring, passing on knowledge, manufacturing, teaching: these functions should be protected when they are carried out on a small scale with direct value for the community.

Pure capitalism tells us that everything must be maximally profitable. Pure communism, for its part, often denied the force of individual initiative. Between the two, there may be a more human path: a society that keeps the energy to create, but refuses to let that energy turn into a right to exploit.

I do not believe we should automatically admire great fortunes. I believe, rather, that we should admire systems capable of preventing them from forming at the cost of the exhaustion of others. A mature society should not measure its success only by the number of billionaires it produces, but by its ability to allow ordinary people to live with dignity, to learn a trade, to pass it on, and to contribute to shared prosperity without being crushed.

Real progress does not consist only in producing more. It consists in producing differently. In producing without humiliating. In producing without crushing. In producing without making the silent suffering of some the spectacular success of others.

Capitalism may still have a place. But it needs a counterweight. And that counterweight should be solidarity, practical education, and active protection for small trades against the grip of the logic of accumulation.

Because a society does not become better when a few rise very high. It becomes better when the greatest number can finally stand upright.
