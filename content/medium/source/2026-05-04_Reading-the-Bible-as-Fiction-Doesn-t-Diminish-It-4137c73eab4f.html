Reading the Bible as Fiction Doesn’t Diminish It

Beyond miracles, the Bible’s moral power still speaks through our modern myths, heroes, and stories.

Reading the Bible as Fiction Does Not Diminish It

What miracles, superpowers, and great stories can still teach us

I do not believe a man can walk on water.

I do not believe a man can turn water into wine.

I do not believe a man can raise the dead.

But that does not prevent me in any way from admiring the moral message of Jesus.

On the contrary.

I think we can disbelieve in miracles and still recognize the power of a story. We can read the Bible as a foundational fiction without reducing it to “just a story.” We can doubt that an event happened literally as it is told, while still asking: what is this story trying to teach us about how to live?

That is the question that interests me.

Not because the debate over miracles is useless. It may be important for believers, theologians, historians, philosophers, or anyone who reflects on the religious truth of Christianity.

But that is not the debate I want to have here.

What interests me is the moral core of the story.

And that core remains powerful, even when one no longer believes in miracles literally.

What makes us tune out today made people believe then

Today, if I say I know someone who can walk on water, I do not make that person more credible. I make my own story less credible.

If I say someone can turn water into wine, heal the sick by laying hands on them, or raise the dead, many people will stop believing me. In our modern imagination, miracles are often received as exaggeration, legend, manipulation, or fiction.

But in the time of Jesus, it was the opposite polarity.

In a religious world, miracles could add credibility to a prophet. To say that a man healed the sick, cast out demons, multiplied loaves, or raised the dead was not necessarily to make him suspicious. On the contrary, it was a way of suggesting that he might be a messenger of God.

What makes us tune out today could make people believe then.

The same narrative element — the miracle — therefore does not produce the same effect depending on the cultural world in which it is received.

For a modern reader, a miracle may weaken the story. For an ancient religious audience, it could strengthen it.

But independently of that debate, something remains.

The message remains.

One can disbelieve that Jesus walked on water and still admire his call to move beyond fear.

One can disbelieve that he turned water into wine and still be touched by the image of abundance, joy, and the transformation of lack.

One can disbelieve in the resurrection as a historical event and still be moved by what the story carries: injustice, sacrifice, hope, forgiveness, the victory of love over hatred.

The question is therefore not only: “Did this happen?”

The question also becomes: “What way of living does this story propose to us?”

Fiction can contain moral truth

The word “fiction” is often misunderstood.

When we say that a story is fiction, people sometimes hear: “it is only a story.” As if fiction were automatically inferior to fact. As if it could not contain truth.

I think exactly the opposite.

A fiction can be false in the literal sense and true in the human sense.

It may not describe a historical event and still reveal something profound about fear, violence, guilt, loyalty, love, betrayal, sacrifice, or forgiveness.

Fiction can console. It can warn. It can shape a conscience. It can offer models. It can also offer anti-models. It can help us see more clearly what we all experience, even when the story itself is impossible.

Reading the Bible as fiction does not mean despising it.

It means reading it as a great symbolic story: with its extraordinary characters, impossible scenes, powerful images, moral tensions, and lessons for life.

For believers, these stories may be true in the religious sense.

For non-believers, they may remain true in the moral sense.

That shared space is precisely what interests me.

Before superheroes, there were already extraordinary figures

If we read the Bible as a great symbolic story, one thing becomes striking: it is full of extraordinary characters.

Before Marvel and its superheroes, there was already the Bible and the religious imagination, with its prophets, angels, chosen ones, demons, healers, saviors, and miraculous figures.

Before Superman and his extraordinary strength, there was Samson and his superhuman strength.

Before the Jedi, sensitive to an invisible power flowing through the world, there were the prophets who claimed to hear the word of God.

Before modern heroes who receive an impossible mission, there was Noah.

Noah is not merely the one who survives the Flood. His extraordinary power, if we read the story with our modern imagination, is to hear God, receive implausible instructions, and build an ark before the catastrophe is visible.

His power is to hear what no one else hears, to believe what no one else believes yet, and then to act before others understand.

That is absolutely extraordinary.

Almost superheroic.

But the point of these stories is not only the supernatural. The point is what these powers reveal.

What should one do with one’s strength? How should one use one’s power? How should one respond to offense? How can one avoid living in fear? How can one forgive? How can one love? How can one avoid becoming what wounded us?

These questions are not only religious.

They are human.

Power reveals who we are

In biblical stories as in modern fiction, power is never merely spectacular.

It is moral.

Samson has immense strength, but that strength does not automatically make him wise. Noah hears God, but that ability forces him to act against all visible evidence. Jesus performs miracles, but he puts them at the service of the sick, the excluded, the poor, the grieving, and the guilty.

The miracle is therefore not merely a display of power. It reveals a value: compassion, care, hope, healing, abundance, uplift.

The same is true in modern fiction.

Spider-Man raises the question of responsibility. Superman raises the question of power restrained by goodness. Batman raises the question of a wound that can become either mission or obsession. Harry Potter raises the question of love in the face of fear and death. Anakin raises the question of immense potential destroyed by fear.

Power has no value in itself.

Everything depends on what it serves.

That is what distinguishes the hero from the tyrant.

The moral pivot of Jesus

What strikes me about the figure of Jesus is not only that he performs miracles in the story.

It is the moral shift he brings about.

A significant part of the older biblical imagination is marked by divine justice, fault, and punishment. Adam and Eve are expelled from Paradise. Sodom is destroyed. The Flood wipes out almost all of humanity. The plagues strike Egypt. The law can lead to stoning. God often appears as a power to be feared, a judge before whom human beings must tremble.

This must obviously be nuanced.

The Old Testament also contains tenderness, mercy, faithfulness, poetry, love, and compassion. It is not a uniform block of violence and punishment.

But in the common imagination, the movement toward Jesus still represents a major pivot.

With Jesus, the center of gravity shifts.

God is no longer only the one we fear. He becomes the one we love.

He is no longer only the judge who punishes. He becomes the father who welcomes.

He is no longer only the power that condemns. He becomes the presence that forgives, protects, heals, and raises up.

Jesus does not emphasize vengeance, but forgiveness. He does not go only toward the pure, but toward the excluded. He does not merely ask us to obey the law, but to understand its spirit. He does not say only to fear God, but to love God. He does not answer violence with violence, but with a form of moral disarmament.

That is what remains powerful, even when one does not believe in miracles.

The miracle can be debated.

The moral message remains accessible.

And that message is profoundly revolutionary: not to live in fear, not to be governed by hatred, not to respond mechanically to offense with revenge, not to reduce a person to their fault, to protect rather than punish, to love rather than dominate.

I have the right not to believe that a man can walk on water.

But that does not prevent me from deeply admiring the message of love, peace, and forgiveness attributed to Jesus.

Great stories give us models and anti-models

Great stories do not only give us heroes to admire.

They also give us figures we should not become.

Jesus embodies love, forgiveness, peace, and the refusal of vengeance. Noah embodies listening and radical trust. Moses embodies mission, liberation, and perseverance despite doubt.

Conversely, Cain shows jealousy turning into violence. Pharaoh shows pride that refuses to yield. Judas shows betrayal. Samson shows that immense strength does not necessarily protect us from impulse and downfall.

Modern fiction does the same.

Spider-Man embodies responsibility. Superman embodies power at the service of the weak. Luke Skywalker embodies the refusal to give in to hatred. Harry Potter embodies love, courage, and sacrifice.

Conversely, Anakin shows fear becoming possession, then violence. Voldemort shows the refusal of death, vulnerability, and love. Palpatine shows pure manipulation.

These characters exist to make us think.

They ask us: in this situation, who am I becoming?

Jesus, Yoda, and fear

Yoda is not Jesus.

Star Wars is not the Gospel.

But both stories understand that fear can corrupt the soul.

In Star Wars, Yoda teaches that fear leads to anger, anger leads to hatred, and hatred leads to suffering.

That is the tragedy of Anakin Skywalker.

Anakin does not fall simply because he is evil. He falls because he is afraid of losing what he loves. His fear becomes possessive. Possession becomes anger. Anger becomes violence. And violence eventually turns him into the very thing he wanted to fight.

In the Gospels, the message of Jesus moves in another direction: do not live in fear, do not answer violence with violence, love rather than hate, forgive rather than allow yourself to be possessed by revenge.

In Star Wars, fear becomes the dark side.

In the Gospels, fear, hatred, and revenge are overcome by love, forgiveness, and trust.

These are not the same stories. They do not have the same status. They do not carry the same religious depth.

But they work from a common intuition: what governs us inwardly eventually transforms us.

Fear can transform us.

Hatred can transform us.

Love can, too.

Our modern fictions as collective moral stories

Marvel, Star Wars, Harry Potter, and The Lord of the Rings are not merely entertainment. They are modern fictional universes with their characters, symbols, conflicts, significant objects, lineages, rules, communities, and debates of interpretation.

They are not religions in the strict sense. But they sometimes fulfill certain cultural functions that great religious stories once fulfilled.

They offer models and anti-models. They tell stories of good and evil. They stage temptation, fall, sacrifice, and redemption. They give millions of people a shared language.

No one literally believes that Spider-Man protects New York, that the Jedi existed, or that Hogwarts sends letters to wizard children.

But many people symbolically inhabit these stories.

And that is where their power begins.

The word “mythology” can be useful here, as long as we understand it properly. I do not use it to mean “lie.” I use it in a cultural sense: a set of stories that gives a community images, characters, models, warnings, and a way of thinking about good and evil.

In that sense, the Bible can be read as a foundational mythology, while Marvel, Star Wars, or Harry Potter can be read as modern popular mythologies — without implying that they have the same status.

The Bible is tied to a religious tradition, to centuries of faith, liturgy, theology, community, power, and transmission.

Modern fictional universes are tied to cultural industries, studios, platforms, brands, and intellectual property.

The comparison therefore has limits.

But it remains illuminating.

Because it shows that we have not stopped producing stories larger than ourselves.

We have simply changed the forms, the media, and the places where these stories circulate.

An intuition that does not come from nowhere

This intuition is not entirely new.

Roland Barthes, in Mythologies, showed that modern societies continue to produce myths through media, images, and popular culture.

Umberto Eco analyzed Superman as a modern mythical figure.

Joseph Campbell, with his idea of the hero’s journey, influenced much of the way we read popular stories, especially Star Wars.

John Shelton Lawrence and Robert Jewett analyzed the superhero as a figure of salvation in the American imagination.

Grant Morrison presented superheroes as embodied ideas: Superman, Batman, or the X-Men are not only commercial characters, but symbols of moral, psychological, and collective forces.

Henry Jenkins, for his part, showed how contemporary fictional universes extend into fan communities, debates, games, costumes, theories, and reinterpretations.

These authors do not all say exactly the same thing. But they converge toward a common idea: modern societies have not stopped producing collective stories charged with meaning.

They produce them differently.

What fiction still allows us to share

This is where reading the Bible as fiction becomes valuable.

If we remain only in the debate over whether miracles are real or not, believers and non-believers risk no longer speaking to each other.

The believer defends the sacred truth of the story. The non-believer rejects what they consider impossible. And each remains in their own camp.

But if we also accept reading the Bible as a foundational fiction, a shared space opens up.

The believer may continue to see it as the word of God.

The non-believer may see it as a great moral story.

Both can discuss what the text proposes as a way of living.

This is where fiction becomes a bridge.

It does not necessarily demand literal adherence.

It demands attention.

It asks us to pose another question:

What does this story show us about ourselves?

What does it invite us to become?

What does it warn us not to become?

This is also what great contemporary fictional universes do.

One can disbelieve in the Force and still understand what Star Wars tells us about fear.

One can disbelieve in Superman and still admire the idea of power placed at the service of the weak.

One can disbelieve in Spider-Man and believe deeply that great power comes with great responsibility.

One can disbelieve in Harry Potter and understand that love protects better than domination.

One can disbelieve in the miracles of Jesus and radically admire his message of love, peace, and forgiveness.

Fiction is therefore not the enemy of moral truth.

It is often its vehicle.

What remains when we no longer believe in miracles

We live in an age less inclined to believe in ancient miracles.

For many people, supernatural stories no longer convince. They may even distance us from the message they were meant to carry.

But it would be a mistake to throw away the message with the miracle.

One can disbelieve that a man walked on water and still be touched by his call to move beyond fear.

One can disbelieve that a man changed water into wine and still see in that gesture an image of abundance, joy, and transformation.

One can disbelieve that a man returned from the dead and still recognize in that story a powerful meditation on injustice, sacrifice, hope, and forgiveness.

In the same way, one can know that superheroes do not exist and still learn something from them.

The Bible, read as fiction, does not become smaller.

It becomes accessible in another way.

It stops being only an object of belief or disbelief.

It also becomes a reservoir of stories, symbols, and moral questions.

Behind fiction, there are sometimes very real values.

And perhaps that is what matters most.
