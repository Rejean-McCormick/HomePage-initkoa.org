Logos and Semantic Data: The Same Skeleton of Meaning

“Logos” (λόγος) is a philosophical term associated with account, reason, discourse, and — at its most ambitious — intelligibility: the idea that what is can be understood because it has structure. “Semantic data” is a modern engineering approach to meaning: representing concepts and their relations explicitly so machines can operate on them.

They are not the same thing. But they converge on a deep structural idea:

Meaning is not primarily in isolated words. Meaning is carried by relations, constrained by rules, and made intelligible through what follows from what.

This article clarifies that overlap, then updates a common claim about semantic systems: while “semantic data” has often been treated as representational structure only, newer semantic formats increasingly encode parts of epistemic discipline — rules about evidence, ambiguity, traceability, and integrity — inside the format itself.

Terms that otherwise blur

Logos

In this article, logos means: a reasoned account — an intelligible explanation whose parts hang together through relations of reason (support, implication, exclusion, contradiction, explanation).

Logic, reasoning, explanation

In everyday language, “What’s the logic?” often means “What’s the why?” Strictly:

  • Logic: rules of valid inference (what follows from what).
  • Reasoning: inference plus additional premises (definitions, causal claims, principles).
  • Explanation: reasoning aimed at “why,” typically requiring domain assumptions (biology, physics, social dynamics).

A useful bridging phrase for this article is inferential structure: the network of entailments and exclusions that gives an account its internal skeleton.

Semantic data

“Semantic data” means data where meaning is explicit in structure:

  • entities (things),
  • types (kinds of things),
  • relations (how things connect),
  • constraints (what is allowed / disallowed),
  • sometimes inference rules (what follows).

1) Logos as structured intelligibility

Logos is not merely “content.” An account becomes a logos when it is organized:

  • It contains relations among claims (this supports that; this explains that).
  • It contains constraints (some claims cannot coherently stand together).
  • It yields consequences (if these premises are granted, those conclusions follow).

In short:

Logos is intelligibility articulated as an account: meaning stabilized by relations and disciplined by constraints.

This is why logos can be discussed as “information” in a broad sense, while remaining more than a pile of statements: it is information with an internal architecture.

2) Semantic data as meaning moved into structure

Semantic data exists because plain text is semantically costly: interpretation must be reconstructed each time.

Semantic systems move meaning from implicit interpretation into explicit structure:

  • Entities are typed.
  • Relations are explicit and named.
  • Constraints limit what can be asserted.
  • (Sometimes) inference rules derive what follows.

Instead of storing “Marie works at Company X” as a sentence, a semantic representation stores:

  • Person(Marie)
  • Organization(CompanyX)
  • worksFor(Marie, CompanyX)

That is not simply a storage choice. It changes where meaning lives: in relations and constraints, not just in phrases.

3) The overlap: meaning as relational, constraint-governed, consequence-bearing

The logos / semantic-data parallel is structural.

Relational meaning

A word alone is ambiguous; a node alone is inert. Meaning sharpens through a network.

  • Logos: meaning emerges from inferential and explanatory links among claims.
  • Semantic data: meaning emerges from typed relations among entities and concepts.

Constraints as meaning-makers

Constraints prevent semantic collapse:

  • Logos: validity and consistency constrain which inferences are acceptable.
  • Semantic data: ontology/schema constraints constrain what combinations are coherent.

Consequence belongs to meaning

Meaning includes implications:

  • Logos: if A, then B (or not).
  • Semantic data: relations plus rules/constraints yield derivations or contradictions.

This supports the core thesis:

Logos and semantic data share the same skeleton: relations under constraints, with consequences.

4) The divergence: logos is normative; semantic data begins as representational

The resemblance should not be overstated.

Logos is normative

A logos is an account under standards: it can be challenged, defended, corrected, refined. It belongs to practices where reasons are demanded and contradictions matter.

Semantic data is representational by default

A semantic model can be coherent, richly structured, and still be wrong or misleading. Precision does not guarantee truth. Representations always have scope, assumptions, and design choices.

However, it is increasingly inaccurate to say that semantic data “supports norms but doesn’t contain them,” because some semantic formats now encode parts of epistemic discipline directly.

5) Update: semantic formats can embed epistemic discipline

Classic semantic systems emphasized relations, constraints, and (sometimes) inference. Newer semantic formats can package something closer to an “account” by encoding not only meaning structure but also requirements for admissibility, support, and integrity.

A concrete illustration appears in the Kristal v3.x contracts/specification, which shows what a semantic format can support when it treats epistemic discipline as first-class.

No new factual claims without support

A rendering rule can forbid inventing unsupported factual assertions: the renderer “MUST NOT introduce any factual assertion” not supported by an input claim/statement.

Explicit ambiguity preservation

If ambiguity remains unresolved, the system must render it explicitly or refuse; it must not silently pick a single meaning unless the input explicitly resolves it.

Mandatory traceability

Every factual output must have support pointers back to statements/evidence; otherwise it must be omitted, marked uncertain, or fail deterministically.

Evidence and uncertainty as first-class structures

Claims carry explicit confidence/uncertainty structures and evidence fields rather than treating these as optional afterthoughts.

Fail-closed integrity gates

If integrity material is declared and verification fails, the artifact must not be treated as valid.

These are not merely “data fields.” They are rules of admissible account-giving encoded in the format and pipeline.

The corrected statement is:

Semantic systems can encode not only semantics (relations/constraints) but also parts of epistemology (evidence, uncertainty, traceability, refusal behavior, integrity gates) when the format makes these requirements first-class.

Conclusion

Logos and semantic data converge on a shared architecture: meaning as a relational network under constraints, where consequences follow. That is the overlap.

They diverge in the normative dimension: logos is an account under standards of justification and revision, while semantic data is, by default, a representational artifact. Yet newer semantic formats show that the gap can narrow operationally: semantic structures can include not only relations and inference, but also the discipline that makes an account responsible — explicit ambiguity handling, evidence requirements, traceability constraints, and integrity gates.
