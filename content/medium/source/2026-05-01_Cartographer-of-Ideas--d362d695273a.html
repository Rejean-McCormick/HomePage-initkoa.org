Thinking with AI as a Partner

Cartographer of Ideas:

Thinking with AI as a Partner

Artificial intelligence is powerful at searching, organizing, synthesizing, and producing.

It can explore a subject, compare hypotheses, generate paths, structure an argument, propose categories, find analogies, formulate objections, and critique an idea. It can also navigate very complex problems with a certain degree of autonomy.

But it is not perfect.

The more complex an intellectual territory becomes, the more subtle its possible errors can be: a poor category, the wrong level of analysis, a flattened nuance, a seductive but weak analogy, a hidden assumption, an underestimated blocker.

That is where collaboration becomes interesting.

I do not see AI as a simple tool to command, nor as an authority to follow passively. I see it more as a partner in intellectual navigation. A particular kind of partner, of course: it does not have my lived experience, my stakes, my intuition, or my responsibility. But it has other strengths: range, speed, the ability to recombine, compare, classify, produce several angles, and bring up connections I may not have seen on my own.

So the question is not only: What can AI do?

The real question becomes:

How can we think with AI without becoming passive?

For me, the answer begins with a posture: becoming a cartographer of ideas.

Being a cartographer of ideas is not only about looking for answers. It is about learning to navigate information: facts, ideas, concepts, categories, hypotheses, relationships, contradictions, flaws, blockers, and possible paths.

AI and the human mind can then work together to draw a map.

Not a perfect map.
Not a final map.
But a richer map than either could have produced alone.

Two intelligences, two kinds of strengths

AI is powerful at expanding the territory.

It can quickly produce a list of options, synthesize information, compare several frameworks, propose analogies, organize ideas, find counterarguments, and identify possible categories.

It is very useful for opening the field.

The human mind brings something else.

It brings context.
Lived experience.
A sense of what matters.
Intuition shaped by experience.
Judgment about relevance.
The ability to feel that an idea “doesn’t hold.”
Awareness of consequences.
The responsibility to choose.

AI can propose ten maps.
The human can sense which one actually corresponds to the territory they are trying to understand.

AI can generate paths.
The human can recognize which ones are worth following.

AI can find a flaw.
The human can understand why that flaw matters.

The collaboration becomes powerful when we do not ask one to replace the other. We use their respective strengths.

Thinking without social theater

There is also another major advantage: with AI, a large part of ego disappears from intellectual collaboration.

When two humans think together, there are not only ideas at play. There is also self-image, status, the fear of looking ignorant, the fear of hurting the other person, the need to choose one’s words carefully, to protect the relationship, to avoid seeming too slow, too confused, too naïve.

All of that can be legitimate. Human relationships are precious. But this social layer consumes energy.

With AI, much of that burden disappears.

We can say:

I don’t understand.
Explain it more simply.
Start again differently.
Critique my idea.
Assume I am wrong.
Find the flaws.
Destroy this hypothesis if it does not hold.
Start over from the beginning.

We can ask a naïve question without embarrassment. We can expose a confused idea without fearing social judgment. We can ask for ten reformulations. We can ask for a severe critique without hurting anyone. We can admit that we do not understand without losing face.

AI reduces the social cost of ignorance.

That is enormous.

The brain no longer has to protect its image. It can devote more energy to understanding.

This does not make AI superior to a human. It simply makes the relationship different. It creates a space where we can experiment, go backward, simplify, complexify, test hypotheses, ask imperfect questions, and push an idea further without managing the usual social theater.

In this sense, AI becomes a very particular intellectual partner: a partner without an ego to protect, and with whom our own ego can also relax.

Starting with anchor points

When I approach a complex subject with AI, I often start by looking for anchor points.

An anchor point can be a fact, an observation, a constraint, a contradiction, a strong intuition, a principle, or a relationship between two elements.

It is a landmark on the map.

The image of Sudoku is useful. In Sudoku, we do not fill the grid at random. We begin with the cells we are certain about. Those certainties then reduce the field of possibilities. They allow us to move forward logically.

With AI, it is similar.

I can say:

Here are the elements I am relatively certain about.
Treat them as anchor points.
What structures might connect them?
What do they make more probable?
What do they make less probable?
What do they make impossible?

In this collaboration, I often provide the initial landmarks. AI then helps me explore what can be built around them.

I place the points.
AI proposes forms.
I check whether those forms make sense.
It develops them.
I question them.
Together, we refine the map.

This is not a pure command relationship. It is co-navigation.

Revealing forms

A map is not just an accumulation of points. It is a way of seeing the forms between the points.

I like the image of three points determining a circle.

In geometry, three non-collinear points are enough to determine a precise circle. Its center, radius, and position are already implied by the relationship between those points.

The form only has to be revealed.

In thought, something similar can happen. A few well-chosen elements can reveal a larger structure.

But there are not always three points. Sometimes there is only one. Sometimes two. Sometimes ten. Sometimes fifty.

We can imagine a sheet covered with points of different colors. The red points may belong to one logic. The blue points to another. The green points to a third.

The work consists in recognizing which pieces of information belong together, which families emerge, and what form each family draws.

AI is powerful at proposing connections. It can go around a subject and look for similar forms in other domains: economics, biology, law, psychology, architecture, strategy, mathematics.

The human, in turn, can ask:

Does this analogy truly clarify the problem?
Do these points really belong to the same family?
Are we revealing a structure, or forcing a drawing?

A good map reveals.
A bad map imposes.

Zooming in and zooming out

Navigation requires changing scale.

If we zoom in too much, we see the details, but lose the whole.
If we zoom out too much, we see the general structure, but flatten important differences.

Thought works the same way.

We can look at an individual, a group, a region, a society, a system. We can start from a particular case, move up toward a category, then toward a general principle. Then we sometimes have to come back down to the particular to verify whether the principle really holds.

For example, talking about one Quebecer and talking about eight million Quebecers are not the same scale.

Between the two, there are many possible divisions: region, mother tongue, generation, income, profession, social class, level of education, rural or urban environment, relationship to politics.

Each division reveals something.
Each division also hides something.

A category is not just a box. It is a lens.

AI can propose several levels of analysis. The human can sense which one is relevant to the question.

We can work together around questions like:

What do we see if we zoom in?
What do we see if we zoom out?
Which division changes the reading of the problem?
Which category truly clarifies the situation?
Which category distorts it?

Zooming and zooming out are at the heart of intellectual navigation. AI can multiply the angles. The human can help choose the right level of reading.

Taking inventory of the territory

Before choosing a route, we need to know the possible paths.

That is the role of inventory.

In a complex problem, it is often useful to suspend the conclusion and first look at what exists on the map.

What are the resources?
The constraints?
The options?
The risks?
The unknowns?
The dependencies?
The possible paths?
The paths already blocked?

AI is very strong at this work. It can quickly expand the field of possibilities.

But the human can enrich that inventory by adding context, priorities, lived constraints, and practical intuitions.

We can ask:

Take inventory of the territory: resources, constraints, options, risks, unknowns, dependencies, and possible paths.
Do not choose a solution yet.
Help me see the map first.

This step prevents us from confusing the first plausible solution with the best available solution.

When we see only two routes, we think we have to choose between them. After a serious inventory, we may discover that there are ten, twenty, fifty.

AI expands the field.
The human helps recognize what truly matters.

Building the legend: categories and classifications

A raw inventory can become unreadable.

Fifty elements do not yet make a map. They are just fifty elements.

They must be grouped.

Categories then play the role of a legend. They tell us how to read the map.

Which elements belong to the same family?
Which are variations of the same problem?
Which are causes?
Which are consequences?
Which are central?
Which are secondary?
Which are symptoms?
Which are blockers?

AI can propose several classifications. This is one of its great strengths.

But a category is never neutral.

Classifying a population by region does not produce the same map as classifying it by language, income, or generation. Classifying problems by urgency does not give the same view as classifying them by root cause.

A good classification makes the territory readable.
A bad classification distorts it.

The collaboration then becomes very rich:

Propose several ways of classifying these elements.
For each classification, explain what it reveals and what it may hide.

AI produces several possible legends.
The human compares, chooses, adjusts, or rejects them.

Pointing to a flaw

A map can be elegant and false.

An idea can seem coherent while hiding a flaw: a fragile assumption, a contradiction, a blind spot, an unrealistic dependency, missing information.

Pointing to a flaw is not about destroying for the sake of destroying.

It is about marking a place on the map that requires investigation.

There is a difference between saying:

This idea is bad.

And saying:

Here, there is a tension.
Here, there is a hidden assumption.
Here, evidence is missing.
Here, there may be a blocker.

A well-pointed flaw becomes a site of investigation.

AI can find flaws I would not have seen. I can also sense a weakness that AI smoothed over too quickly. That is precisely the value of partnership.

We can ask it:

Here is the current structure.
Point out the possible flaws.
For each flaw, indicate whether it is minor, serious, or potentially fatal.
Tell me what would need to be verified to decide.

Critique then becomes a tool of navigation.

It is not only used to say no. It is used to know where to look more carefully.

Investigating fragile zones

Not all flaws are equal.

Some are simple imprecisions.
Some require correction.
Some reveal an important weakness.
Some show that the whole structure could collapse.

The investigation consists in determining the severity of the flaw.

Is it a difficulty?
A limit?
An exception?
A contradiction?
Missing information?
A risk?
A blocker?

This distinction changes everything.

A difficulty calls for a solution.
A limit calls for adaptation.
An exception calls for precision.
A contradiction calls for reconstruction.
A blocker may require changing routes.

AI can play the role of critic, adversary, comparator, detector of hidden assumptions. The human can play the role of the one who senses the real importance of the flaw, based on context and stakes.

The question is not only:

Is there a flaw?

But:

Which flaw truly threatens the structure?
Which flaw is secondary?
Which flaw requires investigation?
Which flaw changes the status of the idea?

In fragile zones, human-AI collaboration becomes especially valuable.

Distinguishing difficulty from blocker

One of the most important moves is distinguishing a difficulty from a blocker.

A difficulty complicates the path.
A blocker can close the path.

There are variable blockers: those that can be bypassed, moved, reduced, negotiated, or solved.

And there are invariable blockers: those that impose a fundamental limit under current conditions.

An invariable blocker may be a logical contradiction, a physical impossibility, an absolute legal constraint, an impossible dependency, an inaccessible resource, or an initial assumption that turns out to be false.

At that point, optimization is not enough. The status of the idea must change.

The project can continue.
It can be modified.
It can be suspended.
It can be abandoned.

A good map does not only show paths. It also shows walls, cliffs, and closed roads.

AI can signal these obstacles. The human can help judge their real importance.

Keeping some routes dormant

A blocked route is not always a nonexistent route.

Some ideas are impossible under current conditions, but not impossible forever.

They may depend on a technology that does not yet exist, a legal change, an inaccessible resource, missing information, or an unexpected solution.

In that case, the idea does not necessarily have to be erased.

It can be put into dormancy.

Impossible for now.
Blockers identified.
Reconsider if conditions change.

This is different from abandonment.

To abandon is to remove the idea from the active map.
To suspend is to recognize that it is not viable now, but that it could become viable later.

The cartographer of ideas does not confuse “impossible now” with “absolutely impossible.”

They sometimes keep the trace of a closed passage, because one day that passage may open.

Giving ideas a status

After exploring, taking inventory, classifying, zooming in, zooming out, pointing to flaws, and identifying blockers, ideas must be given a status.

An idea can be viable.
Viable with modifications.
Fragile but recoverable.
Promising but incomplete.
To be verified.
Blocked.
Suspended.
Non-viable.
To be abandoned.

This step is essential.

The goal is not to save every idea. Nor is it to destroy them too quickly.

The goal is to understand their real condition.

Some ideas must be developed.
Some must be corrected.
Some must be tested.
Some must sleep.
Some must fall.

AI helps analyze.
The human helps judge.
Together, they can better decide the next path.

Collaborating without becoming passive

The danger with AI is not only that it can be wrong.

The danger is that the human becomes passive.

If we merely receive content, the mind becomes an organ of digestion. We read, accept, reformulate, and move on.

But collaboration can be much richer.

We give anchor points.
AI proposes forms.
We ask for several maps.
AI produces classifications.
We zoom in.
We zoom out.
We point to a flaw.
AI investigates it.
It finds a blocker.
We judge its importance.
An idea changes status.

This is not a master-tool relationship.
Nor is it complete delegation.

It is co-navigation.

Each brings its strengths.

Conclusion: thinking with two intelligences

Artificial intelligence forces us to clarify the role of our own intelligence.

If AI can search, organize, synthesize, and produce, our value does not consist only in producing one more answer.

It consists in collaborating differently.

Knowing how to place anchor points.
Knowing how to zoom in and zoom out.
Knowing how to ask for an inventory of the territory.
Knowing how to compare several categories.
Knowing how to point to a flaw.
Knowing how to investigate a fragile zone.
Knowing how to distinguish a difficulty from a blocker.
Knowing how to keep certain ideas dormant.
Knowing how to decide which routes deserve to be followed.

That, for me, is what it means to be a cartographer of ideas.

Thinking with AI is not abandoning one’s intelligence.
Nor is it simply directing a machine.

It is learning to think in partnership.

AI can expand the map.
The human can add context, judgment, intuition, and a sense of priorities.

Together, they can better navigate the space of ideas.

Perhaps this is one of the great promises of AI: not to replace the human mind, but to offer it a partner without ego, fast, vast, imperfect, available, with whom it becomes possible to map the complex territories of thought more freely.
