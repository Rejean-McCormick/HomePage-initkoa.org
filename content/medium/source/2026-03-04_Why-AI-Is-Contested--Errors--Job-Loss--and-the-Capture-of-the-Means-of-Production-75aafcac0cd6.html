Why AI Is Contested: Errors, Job Loss, and the Capture of the Means of Production

Artificial intelligence is contested for two main reasons. First, it makes mistakes. Second, it takes jobs away from humans. These criticisms do not have the same weight. Mistakes are largely a question of method and verification. Job loss is a structural question of power, because when work is automated, value shifts toward whoever owns and controls the tool — toward whoever owns the means of production.

1) Errors: AI can self-critique, but iteration without new anchors eventually drifts

AI makes errors. It can also critique its own output when you feed it back: you can ask it to identify mistakes, explain what it could have done better, and propose an improved version. This can produce real gains.

But this process cannot be repeated indefinitely without adding new information. If you keep asking “improve this” without improving the context, the changes become less and less grounded. The model may start optimizing for “sounding better” rather than staying faithful to the original intent, or it may introduce new assumptions to justify changes. At that point, iteration stops converging and starts drifting.

What stabilizes improvement is not “more iterations,” but better anchors: clearer requirements, fuller context, and external signals that can confirm what is actually wrong.

Example: building an application

In software creation, multiple files can be generated with scattered mistakes. If the AI only sees one file at a time, it may fix something locally while missing the real cause elsewhere. But if you provide:

  • the complete code dump (all files, structure, dependencies),
  • a system-generated error log (exceptions, stack traces),
  • and ideally tests or explicit requirements,

then the correction becomes well-defined. The AI can see how files relate to each other, and the logs show what fails in reality. In this setting, errors are not a reason to reject AI outright; they are a reason to treat AI as an accelerator inside a workflow: context → diagnosis → fix → validation.

2) AI takes jobs: automation shifts value toward tool owners

The most serious criticism is not that AI makes mistakes. The most serious criticism is that AI removes human jobs or drastically reduces the human hours needed to produce the same output.

When an organization replaces human labor with automated output, income that used to be distributed as wages tends to shift into other forms of captured value: profits, rents, and control. This is why job loss is not just an economic side effect — it is a political and social issue.

History offers precedents. Machines have replaced entire categories of work before. Automated looms, for example, enabled a scale and abundance of clothing that would have been impossible if everything remained handwoven. Abundance can be a real good: it reduces scarcity and expands access.

But increased productivity is not automatically social progress. Automation becomes socially acceptable only under a strict condition: the benefit must be global for humanity, not a mechanism for further enrichment of a minority.

3) Job loss connects directly to the concentration of the means of production

This is where the second criticism — job loss — links directly to the capture of the means of production.

When AI takes jobs, the value created does not vanish. It changes hands. The decisive question becomes: who owns the AI, who controls access to it, and who collects the productivity gains.

If AI becomes a means of production controlled by a small group, it can reinforce economic domination: wealth concentrates, inequality grows, and dependence increases. In that scenario, the contestation of AI is not irrational fear. It is a rational reaction to a future where automation removes jobs while the benefits flow upward.

4) Creating to serve humanity: transmit a message, and accept being surpassed

A separate dimension of this debate is creation. Many creators fear replacement or devaluation — understandably, because creative work is already economically fragile.

But there is also an approach to AI use that is not about crushing artists and not about personal profit as the final aim. The aim can be to produce work in service of humanity, to transmit a message, and to put material into the world that others can pick up, reinterpret, and improve.

In that view, the goal is not to protect an “original” as untouchable. The goal is for real artists to take what exists and make it better — so that, years later, the initial version might be heard as a first step rather than a final achievement. Being surpassed is not failure; it is evidence that the work functioned as a platform.

And if AI increases collective prosperity — if that prosperity is truly shared — then society can better support artists and fund the long time horizons that meaningful creation requires.

Conclusion: AI should be judged by how its benefits are distributed

AI errors are managed through context, constraints, and validation: that is primarily a methodological issue. Job loss is a structural issue: it shifts power toward whoever owns the tool. That is why the criticism “AI takes jobs” is inseparable from the question of who controls the means of production.

AI becomes acceptable when its benefits are global, shared, and oriented toward humanity. It becomes dangerous when it concentrates power, widens the gap between rich and poor, and turns automation into a mechanism of domination.

The decisive debate is not “for or against AI.” The decisive debate is: who controls it, and who receives its fruits.
