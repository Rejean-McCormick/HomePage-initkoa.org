Beyond the State: Media, Networks, Code, and the Fragmentation of Religious Power

Power is still often described as if it resided primarily within the state. We think in terms of legislative, executive, and judicial authority. That structure remains real, necessary, and fundamental. But it is no longer sufficient to describe what actually governs contemporary societies.

A society is not guided only by those who write the laws, those who enforce them, or those who interpret them. It is also shaped by what frames its perception of reality, by what legitimizes its moral judgments, by what amplifies or distorts public speech, and by what makes certain actions possible, fluid, and automatic while others remain theoretical or impractical.

In other words, modern power is less concentrated than it once was. It is distributed across multiple layers.

Alongside the three institutional powers, we must now take seriously the fourth power of the media, the fifth power of the networked public, and a newer, quieter but increasingly decisive force: the power of code — platforms, programs, protocols, and digital infrastructures that organize an ever-growing part of collective life.

Within this landscape, religion raises a particular question. It no longer occupies the position it held for centuries. Yet the type of power it once exercised has not disappeared. It has transformed. It has fragmented. It has been redistributed.

Understanding this transformation is essential if we want to think clearly about governance today.

Visible Powers and Diffuse Powers

The first three powers have a clear form.

The legislative power consists of politicians — those who draft, debate, and vote on laws.
The executive power consists of those who implement political authority — government, administration, military, and police.
The judicial power consists of judges and courts — those who interpret the law, arbitrate disputes, and determine what conforms to legal order.

This architecture remains essential. Yet between written law and the actual conduct of a society, other forces intervene.

Power must be narrated.
It must be perceived.
It must appear just — or at least acceptable.
It must be relayed, contested, amplified, or restrained.
And increasingly, it must be encoded into systems that organize concrete behavior.

A society never responds only to legal texts. It also responds to narratives, symbols, attention flows, technical architectures, reputation systems, and often implicit moral frameworks.

For this reason, the classical theory of three powers must now be complemented.

The Fourth Power: Shaping Public Perception

The fourth power is the power of the media.

Historically it referred to the press. Today it must be understood more broadly: newspapers, television, radio, digital media, news networks, distribution platforms, editorial logics, and visibility mechanisms that determine what will be seen, in what order, and with what emotional tone.

The fourth power does not govern directly. It governs attention.

It determines what appears urgent.
It defines what seems important.
It turns certain events into headlines while others fade into background noise.
It provides the language through which the present is interpreted.

That is its power. A population that perceives the world through a certain media framing does not inhabit exactly the same reality as another. Facts may be identical, but their intelligibility is never entirely the same.

For this reason, a free press is not a decorative element of democracy. It is a mechanism of accountability. It forces power to answer. It prevents governments and institutions from becoming the sole narrators of their own actions. It preserves a public memory capable of resisting erasure, forgetting, or revision.

But this power also has its darker side. When the fourth power stops informing and begins primarily capturing attention, accelerating outrage, polarizing audiences, and dramatizing events, it no longer strengthens collective understanding. It weakens it.

The Fifth Power: The Networked Public

The fifth power, in its most widely recognized modern meaning, refers to the networked public: connected citizens, bloggers, activists, whistleblowers, digital communities, and audiences capable of monitoring, correcting, contesting, or amplifying institutional and media narratives.

This power is not centralized. It is distributed, mobile, and turbulent.

It can expose blind spots in journalism.
It can reveal abuses.
It can document what institutions prefer to hide.
But it can also distort, simplify, harass, polarize, and propagate destructive waves of collective emotion.

The fifth power is therefore not the wisdom of the crowd. It is the capacity of connected citizens to intervene in the public sphere.

This marks a profound transformation. For a long time, the population mainly received narratives. Today it relays them, modifies them, saturates them, contests them, and sometimes turns them viral. Public speech is no longer simply top-down. It circulates unpredictably — sometimes constructive, sometimes corrosive.

The fifth power does not replace the fourth. It places it under constant pressure, creating competition between information, counter-information, perception, emotion, and interpretation.

The Sixth Power: Code

Another layer has emerged — quieter but structurally powerful: the power of code.

Code does not merely describe the world. It organizes the behaviors possible within digital environments, and increasingly within social life itself.

A platform determines what becomes visible.
A protocol determines what is permitted.
A recommendation system determines what is amplified.
An interface determines what feels natural, difficult, encouraged, or discouraged.
A workflow determines how organizations communicate, validate decisions, archive actions, and execute responsibilities.

Code acts as a form of power because it transforms abstract decisions into concrete conditions of existence.

Where speech persuades, code configures.
Where morality advises, code constrains.
Where media influences, code administers.

It determines permissions, frictions, thresholds, access, priorities, memory, and even the rhythm of action. What is not encoded often remains intention. What is encoded becomes procedure, habit, automation, environment.

For a long time we treated code as a technical tool. It must now also be understood as a structural power that organizes reality.

And What About Religion?

Religion does not fit neatly into the modern numbering of powers. It is neither the fourth, nor the fifth, nor the sixth power in the commonly accepted sense.

Yet it has not ceased to be politically significant.

Historically, religion carried several functions that are now distributed across different spheres of society.

It offered a moral framework.
It provided a cosmology.
It proposed exemplary figures.
It structured concepts such as guilt, redemption, sacrifice, justice, vocation, and limits.
It established rituals, symbols, and shared narratives.
It could also legitimize — or delegitimize — political authority.

Religion was not merely a system of belief. It was an infrastructure of cohesion and legitimacy.

This integrative function has weakened in modern societies.

Religious Power Did Not Disappear. It Fragmented.

The crucial point is this: the power once concentrated in religion did not vanish. It was redistributed.

The moral framework now appears in ideologies, human-rights discourse, activist causes, humanitarian narratives, and cultural orthodoxies.

The mythological and heroic dimension has migrated into cultural industries — cinema, television, fictional universes, national narratives, and even political storytelling.

Symbolic cohesion has fragmented into subcultures, emotional communities, digital tribes, ideological affiliations, and media identities.

Authority over truth has partly shifted toward science, universities, institutions, experts — or their imitations.

Religion once formed an integrated symbolic system. The contemporary world has distributed its functions across multiple domains of power.

What sometimes feels like a loss of meaning may actually be a dispersion of meaning.

Mythology Did Not Disappear. It Diversified.

It would be incorrect to think that modern societies have simply abandoned mythology.

Major religions once offered exemplary figures, founding narratives, struggles between good and evil, journeys through trial, sacrifice, revelation, fall, and redemption. They provided models of conduct and a shared symbolic universe.

That function has not disappeared. It has diversified.

Today symbolic references emerge from multiple mythologies: the Bible for some, but also Star Wars, The Matrix, Harry Potter, superhero universes, and many other collective narratives. The forms change, the media change, the cosmologies change — but one constant remains: human beings continue to orient themselves through figures of exception, stories of transformation, and heroic models.

These narratives present individuals endowed with unusual insight or power: prophets, chosen ones, saviors, initiates, rebels, protectors — figures who see what others cannot see, endure what others cannot bear, or cross a trial that reshapes the shared world. Such figures become symbolic markers. They provide images of justice, courage, responsibility, sacrifice, loyalty, transgression, and redemption.

These stories are not merely entertainment. They are symbolic reservoirs from which populations draw representations of what deserves admiration, fear, imitation, or resistance.

The decisive difference with historical religions lies elsewhere: modern mythologies rarely unify an entire civilization under a single symbolic horizon. They gather people, but in fragmented ways. Where a dominant religion once imposed a shared symbolic sky, contemporary mythologies distribute identification across many competing universes.

In other words, mythology remains — but it no longer produces the same unity.

Living After Symbolic Unity

The central issue is therefore not that religion has disappeared. The issue is that we now live after the symbolic unity it once helped produce.

We possess immense media power.
We possess unprecedented connective power.
We possess rapidly expanding technological power.

We know how to broadcast, connect, profile, recommend, automate, archive, mobilize, and segment.

But we share a common symbolic horizon far less easily.

We multiplied channels but lost the center.
We increased our capacity to act without guaranteeing our capacity to orient ourselves.
We refined our tools while dispersing our shared references.

This helps explain why contemporary conflicts amplify so easily. The fourth power accelerates perception. The fifth power amplifies or fragments it. The sixth power structures behavior. Yet the symbolic cohesion that might restrain escalation, order values, or sustain shared limits is often fragmented, contested, or absent.

The problem is therefore not merely technical or media-driven. It is civilizational.

Why This Matters for Governance

A society does not hold together only through institutions. It also depends on what allows people to judge, interpret, limit themselves, recognize what deserves protection, and imagine a common good that exceeds immediate impulse.

If the fourth power becomes pure attention capture, it stops illuminating.
If the fifth power becomes pure turbulence, it stops correcting.
If the sixth power becomes pure optimization, it stops serving human ends.

And if no symbolic layer remains capable of linking people through shared models, limits, responsibilities, and images of justice, then society becomes extraordinarily powerful — and deeply disoriented.

The governance of the future cannot therefore rely on the state alone. It must articulate several forces simultaneously: media accountability, the vigilance of the networked public, the responsibility embedded in code, and a renewed symbolic maturity capable of connecting people without dominating them.

Without such balance, we risk building societies that are technologically sophisticated yet morally centrifugal.

Conclusion

It is no longer sufficient to ask who governs.

We must also ask: who narrates, who legitimizes, who amplifies, who configures?

The first three powers still shape the visible structure of the state.
The fourth power shapes attention.
The fifth power shapes circulation, contestation, and amplification.
The sixth power increasingly shapes the field of the possible.

As for religion, it did not simply withdraw. Its former power fragmented and spread across media narratives, cultural mythologies, ideological systems, networked publics, and technological infrastructures.

We now live in an age where power is less concentrated but more pervasive; less sacred but more difficult to discern; less vertical but more deeply embedded in the systems that structure everyday life.

Understanding this distribution of power is not a theoretical luxury. It is a prerequisite for lucid governance.
