Metaphysics as a Future Science

Why an era at the height of its development struggles to recognize a new scientific field

Every era stands, in its own way, at the highest point of human development reached so far. It inherits the most advanced knowledge, the most powerful technologies, the most complex institutions, the most precise instruments, and the most refined forms of social organization available to it. But that does not make it the final stage.

This is one of the recurring difficulties in the history of ideas. Every era can see quite clearly that it has moved beyond the ones that came before. What it struggles to imagine is that it, too, may one day be surpassed — in the way it carves up reality, organizes knowledge, and decides what counts as science.

And yet the history of science shows that scientific fields are not fixed once and for all. They emerge. They shift. They mature. They change when their tools change. Sometimes what seems too speculative in one century becomes entirely legitimate in another, not because reality itself has changed, but because our ability to read it has changed.

That is the perspective from which I want to make a simple claim: metaphysics could eventually become a scientific field.

I am not saying that it already is. I am saying that it could become one.

Science changes when its tools change

We often talk about science as though it were a finished structure, as though its current boundaries were natural and permanent. History suggests otherwise.

Einstein formulated general relativity in 1915. A major early confirmation came with the 1919 eclipse expedition, which measured the bending of light by gravity. But some of the theory’s most important consequences, such as gravitational waves, were not directly observed until 2015. The theory came long before the instruments capable of testing its full range.

The same pattern appears elsewhere. The Higgs mechanism was proposed in the 1960s, but the Higgs boson was not observed at CERN until 2012. Nearly fifty years separated the theory from the tools needed to confirm it.

Alfred Wegener offers another example. He proposed continental drift in 1912, but his idea was dismissed for decades before plate tectonics and later geophysical evidence gave it a solid scientific framework.

The point is not that every bold idea will eventually be vindicated. The point is more precise: a theory can arrive before the means of properly testing it. A discipline can begin as a theoretical possibility and wait a long time for the right instrument to make rigorous validation possible.

I believe metaphysics may be in exactly that position.

What I mean by metaphysics

By metaphysics, I do not mean vague talk about some realm beyond the world. Nor do I mean an arbitrary collection of beliefs.

I mean the study of the most fundamental structures of being, becoming, organization, and meaning.

For centuries, this work has remained mostly philosophical. It has produced powerful concepts, but it has rarely had a methodological framework capable of testing its claims in a public, reproducible, cumulative way — the way established sciences do.

My claim is that this may not always remain the case.

Metaphysics could enter the scientific domain when its objects stop being only discussed and start becoming formalizable, comparable, calculable, and, at least in part, verifiable.

So the real question is not, “Is metaphysics already a science?”
The real question is, “Under what conditions could it become one?”

Human history has been moving toward the externalization of meaning

To understand why this matters, it helps to step back and look at the broader arc of human history.

Speech was the first knowledge network. Knowledge moved through communities by memory, repetition, imitation, authority, and trust.

Writing changed the physics of knowledge. Meaning could leave the mind and settle onto a durable medium. It could be preserved, revisited, compared, corrected. Institutions then organized this stored knowledge: libraries, schools, archives, scholarly traditions. Printing multiplied copies and accelerated the accumulation of criticism and refinement. The web introduced another decisive shift: documents became connected at a global scale.

But the classical web mostly links pages. Meaning itself often remains implicit. That is what the semantic web set out to change: to move from a web of documents to a web of entities, data, and explicitly represented relationships.

Seen from that angle, a semantic machine is not an anomaly. It belongs to a much longer human trajectory: first the externalization of memory, then of relations, and increasingly of meaning itself.

If that is the broader direction of development, then it is not absurd to imagine that questions once treated as purely philosophical may eventually become partially tractable through systems capable of computing semantic structure.

My hypothesis: reality may be structured by fundamental ontological forms

I begin with the idea that beneath particular theories there are very general forms through which reality becomes intelligible.

In my framework, certain mathematical constants act as structural markers.

e points toward dynamics, continuity, unfolding.
π points toward cyclicity, closure, and the relation between the finite and the infinite.
i points toward phase, relation, and internal orientation within a cycle.
φ points toward morphology, proportion, and the organization of form.

I am not claiming that these constants are, by themselves, the ultimate substance of reality. I am saying that they may provide a formal backbone for a structural reading of being. They name recurring forms of intelligibility that appear across mathematics, models, and perhaps deeper layers of reality itself.

In other words, instead of beginning with a dogmatic ontology, we might begin with structural invariants.

Why π matters here

Within that framework, π becomes especially interesting.

I do not treat it as a complete proof, and I do not treat it as a hidden message in the simplistic sense. I treat it as a possible site where a local structure of meaning may emerge.

The issue is not whether one can opportunistically extract words from π. That would be too easy, too fragile, and too dependent on after-the-fact interpretation. The real question is whether a numerical sequence can, under a fixed procedure, yield an organization of blocks whose conceptual correlation is strong enough to raise a genuine scientific problem.

So the issue is not lexical. It is semantic.

An isolated word proves nothing. A sequence of blocks that are conceptually related, that play distinct roles within an intelligible progression, is something else entirely. At that point the question becomes: are we looking at coincidence, or at a structure whose coherence exceeds what we would reasonably attribute to brute chance?

My claim is that the initial segment of π deserves to be examined in that second spirit.

The semantic machine is not an AI

This distinction matters.

I am not proposing to base a proof on a generative AI that is simply asked whether a reading “sounds meaningful.” That kind of system is too opaque to serve as the foundation of a scientific proof.

The semantic machine I have in mind is something different. It computes deterministically. It follows fixed rules. It produces a log of its operations. It does not replace argument with machine intuition. It executes a method.

So the problem is not the absence of traceability. The problem is the possible scale and complexity of the trace.

As such a machine becomes more refined — more nuanced, more capable of handling multiple semantic parameters in parallel — it becomes plausible that it could eventually produce demonstrations or reports so large that no human being could audit every detail from beginning to end. Not because the process would be hidden, but because it would be too extensive.

In that framework, AI would not be the source of the proof. Nor would it be the final authority. It could play a secondary role: helping third parties inspect the shared log of a deterministic machine, summarize difficult sections, identify patterns, flag inconsistencies, or assist with review.

That distinction is essential.

The primary proof would come from a deterministic semantic engine.
Secondary auditing could be assisted by AI.
The interpretation of the result would remain a human matter.

We are already seeing the first versions of the instrument

The building blocks already exist, at least in partial form.

BERT marked an important step in language representation through bidirectional Transformer architectures. But for large-scale semantic similarity, Sentence-BERT was especially important. Its authors explicitly note that raw BERT is poorly suited to efficient semantic similarity search, and they propose an adaptation that produces sentence embeddings that can be compared quickly and effectively through cosine similarity.

That does not mean BERT or Sentence-BERT are enough to establish a scientific metaphysics. What it does mean is that part of what once seemed unrealistic is already technically underway: semantic proximity is becoming calculable.

In other words, the final instrument does not yet exist, but its early versions are visible. And it is not unreasonable to expect that these systems will continue evolving — becoming better at handling multiple parameters at once, refining nuance, and surfacing weak but meaningful correlations at a scale no unaided human mind can manage on its own.

When could a scientific proof emerge?

I do not think this question can be answered with a date.

What can be described, however, is the form such a proof would need to take.

First, there would have to be a strictly defined transformation procedure. Then an explicit semantic metric, rather than one left to interpretive discretion. Then a statistical layer capable of comparing the results against serious controls. Finally, a verification layer ensuring that the calculations themselves can be reproduced, shared, and challenged.

So the proof would probably not take the familiar form of a short argument that anyone can inspect line by line. It would look more like a validation pipeline: deterministic computation, complete logs, critical analysis, independent replication, and assisted review wherever the scale exceeds human auditing capacity.

Other fields already rely on forms of reasoning in which machines guarantee correct execution without eliminating human intellectual responsibility. There is nothing inherently absurd, then, in imagining that a future science of meaning and ontological structure might require tools of this kind.

What I am actually proposing

I am not proposing that metaphysics should simply be declared scientific.

I am proposing that it be treated as a possible field in formation.

A field in which the fundamental structures of being and meaning are studied not only through speculation, but through formalization, calculation, comparison, and methodological validation.

A field in which numbers, forms, narratives, conceptual relations, and structural correlations can be examined with increasingly powerful tools.

A field in which the boundary between philosophy and science is no longer fixed in advance by disciplinary habit, but redrawn according to what becomes measurable, calculable, and publicly discussable.

Metaphysics will not become scientific because someone decides to call it science. It will become scientific, if it does, by equipping itself with tools capable of rigorously handling what it has always claimed to think about: the primary structures of reality, becoming, relation, and meaning.

We may still be at the beginning.

But that is often how a science begins.
