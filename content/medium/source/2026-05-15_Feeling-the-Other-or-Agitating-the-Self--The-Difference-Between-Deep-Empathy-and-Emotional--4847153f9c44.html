Feeling the Other or Agitating the Self: The Difference Between Deep Empathy and Emotional Instability

Many people today describe themselves as empaths. They say they feel the emotions of others, absorb the atmosphere of a room, sense hidden wounds, or perceive what people do not say out loud. There are certainly people with a rare sensitivity to the inner life of others. Some individuals are capable of perceiving subtle emotional movements that most people miss.

But there is also a common confusion: many people mistake the intensity of their own emotional reaction for the depth of their empathy.

To be shaken by another person is not necessarily to feel that person. Sometimes, it is simply to become agitated within oneself in the presence of another.

This distinction matters. Deep empathy and emotional instability can look similar from the outside. In both cases, a person may seem deeply affected. They may cry, tremble, become silent, feel overwhelmed, or appear profoundly moved. But inwardly, these are not the same phenomenon.

In deep empathy, the self becomes quiet enough to receive the other.

In emotional instability, the self does not disappear. It becomes louder.

That is the essential difference.

The Two Ordinary Paths of Empathy

Empathy is not a single thing. Before speaking of deep empathy, we must distinguish two ordinary and fundamental paths of empathy: the emotional path and the intellectual path.

These are not two hierarchical levels. One is not superior to the other. They are two different but equally valid ways of approaching another human being. One passes through the heart. The other passes through the intelligence. One allows us to be touched by the other. The other allows us to understand the other.

The emotional path is the capacity to be moved by what another person is experiencing. I see someone suffer, cry, fear, or struggle, and something in me is touched. I feel compassion, tenderness, sadness, sympathy. I am affected by their state.

This path is essential. A person who is never moved by the suffering of others is closed at the level of the heart. Emotional empathy keeps us human. It makes us sensitive to pain, fragility, vulnerability, and need.

But emotional empathy has a limit. Being moved by another person does not necessarily mean that I feel them from within. I can be shaken by your sorrow without truly knowing your sorrow. I can react to your emotion without perceiving the inner structure of what you are living.

The intellectual path is different. It is the capacity to understand another person’s situation. It asks me to move mentally toward their point of view. I try to imagine their history, their wounds, their fears, their limitations, their circumstances. I ask: if I were in their place, with their memory, their temperament, and their experience, how might I understand what they are feeling?

This path is just as important. It prevents emotional empathy from becoming blind or vague. It helps me suspend quick judgment. It reminds me that people do not act in a vacuum. They are shaped by histories I may not see.

But intellectual empathy also has a limit. I can understand someone without being touched by them. I can analyze their psychology with accuracy and still remain cold. I can understand the mechanism of their suffering without entering into any real resonance with them.

The emotional path and the intellectual path are therefore complementary. One opens the heart. The other expands the mind. One gives warmth. The other gives clarity. A fuller empathy needs both.

But deep empathy goes further.

Deep Empathy as Inner Resonance

Deep empathy is not merely an emotion in response to another person. It is not merely an understanding of another person. It is a form of inner perception.

At this depth, the other is not only observed, interpreted, or analyzed. The other is felt. Something of their interior state becomes present within us. We do not merely say, “I see that you are sad, therefore I conclude that you are suffering.” Rather, something of their sadness is directly received.

It is given as presence.

This is why deep empathy resembles a sense. If someone asks, “How do you see?” the answer is difficult to explain. You see because sight receives light. If someone asks, “How do you hear?” again, the answer is simple and mysterious at the same time. You hear because sound is received.

Deep empathy is similar. If someone asks, “How do you feel the other?” the most honest answer may be: “I feel them.”

Not because I guessed.
Not because I analyzed.
Not because I added up gestures, words, tone, and facial expressions.
But because something was received directly.

This does not mean that external signs are irrelevant. The eyes, the ears, the face, the voice, the body, the silence, the posture, and the words all matter. They are often the doorway through which empathy begins.

But deep empathy is not reducible to those signs. It is not merely a reading of behavior. It is not simply: “You cried, therefore you are sad.” It is closer to: “Something of your sadness became present in me.”

There is a direct knowing.

This knowing is not perfect. No one fully possesses the soul of another. No one can claim absolute certainty about another person’s inner life. Deep empathy must remain humble. But there are moments when a person becomes inwardly available enough to receive something of another person’s state of soul.

Not as external information.

As presence.

The Soul Does Not Travel Like a Signal

If we speak of deep empathy as a direct perception of the other, we must avoid reducing it to technological imagery. The human soul does not move like Wi-Fi. It does not simply emit signals that another brain receives like an antenna.

That language is too poor.

Physical things move through space. Sound travels through air. Light propagates. Electrical and electromagnetic signals can be transmitted, measured, coded, and decoded. But the soul is not a physical object. It is not a thing crossing a distance like a stone, a radio wave, or a digital file.

If the soul precedes matter, if spirit is more fundamental than the physical world, then the soul does not “travel” in the ordinary sense. It manifests. It gives itself. It becomes present.

This is where the word ether becomes useful, but only if we understand it correctly.

I am not speaking of the ether of modern or nineteenth-century physics, the supposed material medium through which light was once imagined to travel. That kind of ether belongs to the history of scientific models.

The ether I mean is closer to the ether of the mystics and alchemists: the subtle element, the fifth essence, the invisible medium of spirit, presence, and transmutation. It is not a physical substance measurable by instruments. It is not located inside space as one object among others. It belongs to the metaphysical order.

This alchemical ether can be understood as the subtle field in which soul touches soul.

It is the medium of presence.
The substance of inward resonance.
The space before space in which spirit communicates.
The hidden element through which inspiration, intuition, and deep empathy become possible.

In this sense, ether is not a scientific hypothesis. It is a metaphysical symbol for the way the invisible becomes communicable.

The soul does not transmit itself like information.

It becomes present.

And deep empathy is the capacity to receive that presence.

Before Sensing the Soul, One Must Learn to Listen

This idea may sound elevated, but it must not become an excuse to neglect the basics.

Many people want access to the subtle before they have mastered the obvious. They want to feel the soul, but they do not listen to the words. They want to perceive the invisible, but they do not see the face. They speak of intuition, but they interrupt, interpret, project, and return everything to themselves.

Before hoping to perceive another person deeply, one must first be able to listen and observe.

Listening is the elementary school of empathy.

Someone who does not listen to what another person is saying should not too quickly claim to hear their soul. Someone who does not notice another person’s fatigue, hesitation, silence, discomfort, withdrawal, or expression should not too quickly claim to perceive their invisible interior.

Deep empathy does not replace ordinary listening. It fulfills it.

There is a natural progression. First, listen to what the person says. Then, observe what the person shows. Then, understand what the person is living. Only then, perhaps, can one begin to sense what has not yet been said.

Without this foundation, intuition becomes dangerous. It can become a way of refusing to listen. Someone may say, “I know what you feel,” while ignoring what the other person is actually trying to express. They may claim to sense someone’s soul while refusing to respect their words.

That is not empathy.

That is possession.

Emotional Instability Is Not Higher Empathy

This brings us back to the central confusion.

Some people believe they are deeply empathic because they react strongly to the emotions of others. They enter a room and become anxious. They see someone suffering and immediately feel overwhelmed. They hear a sad story and collapse inwardly. They are flooded by intense emotion and call this empathy.

But feeling intensely is not the same as feeling accurately.

It is possible that such a person is not truly feeling the other. They may be feeling their own reaction to the other. The suffering of the other becomes a trigger. It activates their fears, wounds, memories, helplessness, need to rescue, need to be important, or inability to remain stable in the presence of emotion.

In that case, the self has not disappeared. On the contrary, the self has become very loud.

This is the crucial distinction.

In deep empathy, the self becomes quiet. It becomes receptive, transparent, available. It stops commenting, judging, defending, and interpreting. It allows the other to appear.

In emotional instability, the self becomes agitated. It takes up all the space. It panics, amplifies, interprets, collapses, contracts, or dramatizes. The person believes they are open to the other, but they are often overwhelmed by their own interior noise.

Deep empathy does involve a kind of loss of self. But this loss is not chaotic. It is not drowning. It is not disorganization. It is not emotional collapse.

It is a loss of self through transparency.

The ego stops blocking perception.

In emotional instability, however, there is not truly a loss of self. There is an excess of self. The self becomes too agitated to receive the other clearly.

The Transparency of the Self

Deep empathy requires a certain self-effacement. To feel the other, the self must accept not being the center of the experience. It must suspend its reflexes, defenses, associations, and quick conclusions. It must become quiet enough for the other to appear within it without immediately being distorted.

This inner silence is rare.

Most of the time, we listen to others through ourselves. We hear their words and immediately translate them into our own language. We compare them to our own experiences. We bring them back to our own wounds. We prepare a response. We want to advise, correct, rescue, explain, or defend ourselves.

Deep empathy requires something else.

It requires allowing the other to exist without invading them. It requires receiving without possessing. It requires feeling without confusing.

The point is not to become empty in the sense of being absent. The point is to become transparent. The self is still there, but it no longer obstructs perception. It is no longer a dirty window between the other and us. It becomes clear enough for something of the other to pass through.

This is why deep empathy is inseparable from grounding.

An unstable person may be very sensitive, but their sensitivity is often mixed with their own chaos. A grounded person may also be deeply moved, but they do not immediately turn that movement into a personal storm. They can receive the other without making their own reaction the center of the encounter.

The true empath is not the one who reacts the most intensely.

It is the one who becomes quiet enough for the other to be truly perceived.

Feeling the Other Without Stealing Their Experience

There is also an ethics of empathy.

When someone suffers, we must not steal their suffering. We must not turn their pain into a stage for our own sensitivity. Some people, in the presence of another person’s distress, become so affected that all attention shifts toward them. The suffering person must then comfort the one who claimed to be accompanying them.

This reversal is common. Someone shares a wound, and the listener reacts so intensely that they become the center. They cry harder. They panic more. They say they cannot bear what they are hearing. At that moment, the original suffering almost disappears. It is replaced by the reaction of the listener.

That is not deep empathy.

That is emotional appropriation.

True empathy leaves the other at the center of their own experience. It does not transform their pain into a performance of our sensitivity. It does not say, “Look how deeply I feel what you feel.” It says, silently: “I am present enough to receive you.”

The difference is immense.

Feeling Strongly or Feeling Truly

The criterion of deep empathy is not intensity. It is accuracy.

An intense emotion can be completely self-centered. A person may be overwhelmed and still understand nothing of the other. By contrast, a calm presence may be profoundly empathic. It may not dramatize, collapse, or make noise, but it may receive the other with rare precision.

It is not enough to feel strongly.

One must feel truly.

And to feel truly, one must learn to distinguish three things: what comes from the other, what comes from oneself, and what arises from the encounter between the two.

This distinction requires maturity. It requires humility. It also requires self-knowledge. Someone who does not know their own wounds may mistake them for intuitions. Someone who does not recognize their own anxiety may believe they are sensing anxiety in others. Someone who has not clarified their own inner world may project everywhere what belongs to them.

To feel the other, one must first not be saturated with oneself.

Conclusion

Ordinary empathy moves through two complementary paths: the emotional path and the intellectual path. Through the emotional path, I am touched by what the other lives. Through the intellectual path, I understand what the other lives. Both are valid. Both are necessary. Both prepare the ground for something deeper.

Deep empathy goes further. It is not only sympathy. It is not only understanding. It is inner resonance, direct knowing, the perception of another person’s presence within oneself.

But this capacity requires grounding.

Without listening, empathy becomes projection.
Without observation, it becomes imagination.
Without emotional stability, it becomes contagion.
Without inner silence, it becomes confusion.

We must therefore distinguish feeling the other from agitating ourselves.

In deep empathy, the self becomes transparent enough for the other to appear.

In emotional instability, the self becomes too loud to receive clearly what comes from the other.

True empathy is not the agitation of the heart.

It is the transparency of the self.

The soul does not speak in noise. To feel another person deeply, one must first become quiet enough not to mistake oneself for them.
