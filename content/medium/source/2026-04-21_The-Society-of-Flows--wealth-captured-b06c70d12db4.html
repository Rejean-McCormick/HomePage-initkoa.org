The Society of Flows: wealth captured

Money and information flow through society; power belongs to those who control the valves, tolls, and choke points.

The Society of Flows

Imagine a gigantic factory.

Pipes run through the building in every direction. Some carry water, others gas, others raw materials, heat, waste, control signals. At every junction, every valve, every filter, something can happen: the flow can be accelerated, slowed, diverted, transformed, measured, taxed, or lost.

Now replace water and gas with two more abstract things: money and information.

Suddenly, modern society becomes easier to read.

Money flows through it like a fluid. Information does too. They enter somewhere, pass through stages, change form, move through institutions, companies, platforms, banks, administrations, and markets. Then they come out somewhere else as goods, services, decisions, profits, debt, technology, reputation, power.

And as in any circulation system, the decisive point is not only what enters the network. It is also what happens in the middle. Who controls the passages. Who owns the valves. Who imposes the tolls. Who takes a cut at every transition.

This may be one of the best ways to understand our era: not as a simple world of property, but as a world of organized flows.

Society as a system of transformation

A society is not just a place where things are owned. It is a system that continuously transforms inputs into outcomes.

We feed into it labor, time, natural resources, energy, skill, attention, knowledge. All of this then passes through a series of stages: firms, bureaucracies, software, banks, schools, hospitals, platforms, logistics systems, legal infrastructures, credit mechanisms, media.

At the output, we get housing, food, care, objects, transport, narratives, social hierarchies, fortunes, dead ends, innovations, and often waste and misery too.

Inside this vast social factory, there are many flows worth tracking. Physical materials, obviously. Energy as well. But two flows are especially important: money and information.

They are not identical. They do not play the same role. But they do share one crucial feature: they make the system operable. And whoever manages to control their circulation gains enormous power.

Money as a flow

We often speak of money as a kind of wealth one possesses. But in everyday life, money behaves more like a current.

It arrives as wages. Then it leaves. It becomes rent, a mortgage payment, groceries, transportation, bills, interest, insurance, taxes, bank fees, subscriptions, repayments, commissions. It moves through a person more than it stays with them.

Seen this way, money looks less like treasure than like a liquid circulating through a network of pipes.

So the real economic question is not only who has a lot of money. It is also where money passes through, what happens to it along the way, and who skims what from its movement.

Take an ordinary example. Someone works forty hours. Their employer pays them. The money lands in their account, then almost immediately flows outward through multiple channels. Some goes to a landlord or a bank. Some to the grocery store. Some to the energy provider. Some to insurance. Some to a range of fees so normalized we barely notice them anymore: monthly charges, interest, commissions, percentages, margins, subscriptions, service fees, access fees, penalties.

Taken one by one, these extractions often seem small. But added up across millions of people, they form a gigantic machine of capture.

To be clear, not every intermediary is bad. A bank can provide a real service. A payment system can make exchange easier. A legal registry, a notarial system, insurance, logistics infrastructure can all be useful, sometimes indispensable.

But there is a fundamental difference between facilitating a flow and living off control of a choke point.

The moment a structure derives its power not mainly from what it produces, but from its position between people and what they need, it stops being just a tool. It becomes a toll booth.

And once enough toll booths are installed throughout the system, the economy gradually stops looking like a mechanism of coordination and starts looking like a mechanism of extraction.

Information as a flow

Information, by contrast, does not primarily serve to buy. It serves to orient.

It tells us where to go, what to do, how to make something, how to avoid an error, how to recognize an opportunity, how to repair a breakdown, how to solve a problem, how to coordinate multiple actors, how to achieve a result with fewer losses.

In that sense, information has immense value even before it is publicly recognized. Knowing where a resource is has value. Knowing how to exploit it has value. Understanding a more efficient process has value. Holding the solution to a difficult problem has value. True information does not stop being valuable simply because it has not yet been socially validated.

But to have effects in the world, information too usually has to pass through a circulation system.

It begins as observation, experience, intuition, investigation, research, know-how. Then it has to be formulated, preserved, transmitted, understood, validated, translated into instructions, protocols, algorithms, procedures, products, strategies, decisions. It moves through schools, laboratories, media, companies, technical teams, networks, platforms, institutions.

Here again, the factory metaphor works. Information enters the system in a raw state, then gets refined, encoded, filtered, amplified, or suppressed. At the output, it becomes something operational.

Much of the modern economy rests on precisely this process. Many firms do not primarily create matter; they create structured information. Design, software, architecture, documentation, standards, supply chains, protocols, branding, organization, accumulated know-how: all of this is information made active.

A manufactured object is not just an arrangement of materials. It is also a vast mass of information crystallized into usable form.

In other words, information is not a secondary add-on to the modern world. It is one of its central conditions.

What money and information have in common

Money and information are not the same thing.

Money is more uniform. It allows value to be measured, stored, and transferred in a common language. Information is more variable. Its worth depends on context, timing, use, and the ability to interpret it. Money is designed to circulate as a general equivalent. Information circulates more erratically: sometimes it matters because it is scarce, sometimes because it is widely shared, sometimes it loses its value the moment it becomes public, and sometimes it only begins to matter once it is distributed.

Yet despite these differences, they play an analogous role in the structure of the system. Both move through channels. Both encounter intermediaries. Both can be withheld, redirected, filtered, slowed, monetized. Both can become tools of coordination or objects of capture.

Money confers power of access.
Information confers power of orientation.

One lets you obtain.
The other lets you know what to do.

And in both cases, decisive power often belongs not to whoever creates the original resource, but to whoever controls the conditions of its circulation.

Power lives at the points of passage

This is where the pipe metaphor becomes politically useful.

In a flow system, you do not need to produce the resource in order to profit from it. Sometimes all you need is control over a strategic point of passage.

Between the worker and their wages.
Between wages and housing.
Between buyer and seller.
Between inventor and market.
Between creator and audience.
Between citizen and administration.
Between discovery and recognition.
Between useful information and the people who could use it.

Whoever occupies that middle position holds a special kind of power. They can allow passage, slow it down, tax it, filter it, rank it, prioritize it, recommend it, hide it, certify it, block it. They can make access smoother or more costly. They can treat their position as a technical function, or turn it into a source of rent.

This may be one of the most uncomfortable truths of our societies: power often comes less from direct creation than from control over interfaces.

Modern wealth is frequently built on the ability to stand between people and what they need.

Siphoning

The word is harsh, but it names something real.

Siphoning is not always dramatic theft. It is not necessarily exceptional. More often it is an ordinary mechanism, built into the routine functioning of the system.

To siphon money is to turn as many human needs as possible into opportunities for extraction. Housing. Food. Transportation. Borrowing. Paying. Studying. Communicating. Healthcare. At each stage, part of the flow can be captured.

To siphon information is to control its distribution, accessibility, discoverability, visibility, credibility. It means arranging things so that what ought to circulate freely only moves through certain paid channels, certain platforms, certain validation devices, certain structures of dependency.

In both cases, the logic is the same: plug into an essential flow and extract as much as possible, as long as the system can bear the drain.

The most troubling part is that this logic eventually starts to look normal. Fees become natural. Commissions become invisible. Rents become respectable. Tolls become the ordinary form of social organization.

And once that normality settles in, we stop asking whether the system is still designed to serve flows, or whether it mainly serves those who live off capturing them.

A moral confusion

At that point, the problem is no longer merely economic. It becomes moral.

We live in a world where success is largely measured by accumulated money. The more wealth someone has managed to capture, the more we assume they must have created value. The more a company succeeds in imposing fees, margins, licenses, subscriptions, commissions, or indispensable positions, the more it is celebrated as efficient, innovative, admirable.

But that equation is misleading.

Accumulating is not necessarily contributing.
Extracting is not necessarily producing.
Controlling a bottleneck is not the same as improving the system.

A society begins to deform itself morally when it values the ability to tax flows more than the ability to feed, heal, teach, repair, build, understand, transmit, or coordinate in genuinely useful ways.

At that point, we start calling mastery of the toll booth “success.”

Meanwhile, activities that are vital but less well positioned within networks of capture remain undervalued. They sustain the system without controlling its most profitable valves.

This is one of the great confusions of modernity: we too easily mistake capture for creation.

Relearning what to look at

Seeing society as a factory crisscrossed by flows does not solve every problem on its own. But it does change the way we look.

Instead of asking only who owns what, we can ask how things circulate.

Instead of focusing only on accumulated wealth, we can examine the channels, interfaces, filters, and choke points.

Instead of assuming that every accumulation reflects an equivalent contribution, we can ask what comes from a real improvement of the system and what comes simply from controlling an unavoidable passage.

This way of thinking sheds light on many contemporary phenomena. It helps explain why so many charges seem tolerable in isolation but crushing in the aggregate. Why so much wealth is built in infrastructure rather than direct production. Why payment systems, platforms, protocols, networks, information monopolies, and credit structures have become so strategic. Why economic suffocation coexists with ever-greater technical sophistication.

Money circulates purchasing power.
Information circulates the power to act.
Both move through systems.
Both can be used in service of collective life, or turned into sites of capture.

So the real question may not simply be: what is this thing worth?
The real question is: who controls its circulation, and what does that control cost everything else?

Because in a factory, what determines the final outcome is not only the quality of the inputs. It is also what gets lost along the way, what gets blocked in transit, and what some actors manage to divert before it reaches its destination.

And in our societies, far more is being diverted than we are willing to admit.
