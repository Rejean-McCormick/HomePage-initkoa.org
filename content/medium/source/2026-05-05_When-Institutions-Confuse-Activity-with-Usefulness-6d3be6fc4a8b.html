When Institutions Confuse Activity with Usefulness

Meetings, galas, training sessions, conferences: unproductive institutional agitation is an ordinary form of abuse of collective resources.

A large room. Oversized video projectors. Stage lighting. A large number of technicians. Catering. Speeches. Applause. Important people seated at the front. Less important people waiting. Opening remarks, tributes, presentations, awards, breaks, official photos.

Officially, it is a professional meeting, a training session, a conference, a summit, an assembly, or a gala.

But one question remains: what was actually produced?

Not simply: did people talk? Did people see one another? Were some people recognized? Was the event pleasant? The real question is more demanding:

Do the concrete results justify the resources consumed?

Time, money, transportation, lodging, food, preparation, logistics, equipment, attention, salaries, person-hours. Once everything is added up, many institutional activities become much harder to defend.

I call this phenomenon unproductive institutional agitation.

What I Mean by Unproductive Institutional Agitation

Unproductive institutional agitation refers to the tendency of public, parapublic, or collectively funded institutions to multiply meetings, training sessions, conferences, summits, galas, travel, and gatherings that consume significant resources while producing few concrete results proportional to the means mobilized.

The problem is not that these activities are always completely useless.

That is precisely what makes them difficult to criticize. They often have partial usefulness. A meeting can help coordinate. A training session can transmit something. A conference can create connections. A gala can recognize people’s work. An in-person meeting can strengthen trust. An event can create a sense of belonging.

But this partial usefulness too often serves as a general justification for disproportionate arrangements.

We no longer ask: what is the reasonable minimum needed to obtain the intended benefit?

Instead, we ask: which room? Which hotel? Which caterer? Which program? Which stage? How many participants? Which speaker? Which recording setup? Which budget? Which delegation? Which social activity?

And the activity expands.

It becomes an event. Then a tradition. Then a budget line. Then an institutional habit.

The Moral Problem: These Are Not Their Resources

Two things must be distinguished.

If a private company decides to organize an expensive party with its own money, one may find it excessive, useless, or poor management. But it is not exactly the same moral problem.

In a public, parapublic, union, school, government, or collectively funded institution, the resources do not simply “belong to the organization.” They are entrusted to the organization for a mission.

They come from taxes, dues, public budgets, mandatory contributions, time paid for by the community, or resources intended to serve a specific mandate.

When these resources are converted into prestige, comfort, socializing, unnecessary travel, or institutional self-congratulation, we are no longer merely dealing with inefficiency. We are dealing with a form of abuse of power.

Not necessarily corruption. Not necessarily something illegal. Not necessarily a spectacular wrongdoing.

Rather, an ordinary abuse, built into the way things are done.

That is precisely what makes it powerful: it is normalized.

Meetingitis: Talking About the Work Instead of Doing It

The most common form of this agitation is meetingitis.

A two-hour meeting with fifteen people is not a two-hour meeting. It is thirty person-hours. If only five people are truly concerned, the other ten are not simply “present.” They are immobilized.

There are meetings where people are informed when a text would have been enough. Meetings where people are consulted even though nothing will change. Meetings where everyone goes around the table so each person can feel they exist. Meetings where some people wait for the discussion to finally concern them. Meetings where decisions are postponed. Meetings where people prepare for other meetings.

Two things are then confused: talking about a problem and acting on that problem.

Institutions produce a great deal of language: agendas, minutes, follow-ups, presentations, reports, committees, subcommittees, consultation tables. All of this creates an impression of activity.

But activity is not action.

An institution can be extremely busy and still be of very little use.

Performative Presence

Another form of unproductive institutional agitation is performative presence.

This is the idea that physical presence automatically gives an activity more value.

People are moved around to listen to a lecture. A team is gathered to read a document. Participants are made to travel for a discussion that could have been held online. A “field” training session is organized where participants passively observe something that could have been shown through photos, video, samples, or a corrected exercise.

Of course, fieldwork can be useful. Physical presence can be essential. Some skills require seeing, touching, comparing, practicing, handling materials, and experiencing the real scale of things.

But this is not always the case.

The problem begins when the location becomes a backdrop of seriousness. When the fact of being there replaces the question of whether it truly adds anything.

We then confuse “more concrete” with “more effective.”

Going into the field can be more concrete. But if participants are merely listening passively to an explanation, the field sometimes becomes educational theater. A photo, a few samples, and a good exercise would have produced almost the same benefit, with far less time and money.

Training as a Pretext

Training is one of the most convenient justifications for unproductive institutional agitation.

Who can be against training?

Training people is necessary. Developing skills is legitimate. Improving practices is desirable.

But many institutional training activities mix real content, a break from the ordinary setting, socializing, travel, meals, comfort, and an impression of professional development.

A full day is organized for content that could fit into one hour. A passive lecture replaces a corrected exercise. A hotel training session replaces a short video. A field activity replaces a simple demonstration. A group meeting replaces a well-constructed document.

The problem is not training. The problem is the disproportion between the educational objective and the structure mobilized.

A good training session should be judged by what it actually changes: skills acquired, mistakes avoided, decisions improved, practices transformed.

Not by the fact that people spent a day together in a pleasant place.

Display Spending

Then there is display spending.

This is money spent not on the real function of an activity, but on its prestige, comfort, décor, or staging.

Hotels, prestigious venues, caterers, lighting, stages, projectors, video recording, hosts, gifts, promotional bags, shows, artists, commemorative objects, decoration, protocol, security, transportation, lodging.

Again, not everything is useless. A room may be necessary. Sound equipment may be necessary. A meal may be reasonable. A certain level of organization may be justified.

But there is a threshold where the institution is no longer paying only for the activity to take place. It is paying to make itself look important.

An administrative meeting treated like a show is not merely a technical expense. It is a message: we are important, we deserve a stage, we deserve a production, we deserve to be showcased.

And if the money comes from collective resources, this showcase becomes morally suspect.

Institutional Self-Congratulation

Another form is institutional self-congratulation.

These are the galas, awards, tributes, ceremonies, and speeches where an institution celebrates its members, its leaders, its mission, its excellence, its innovation, its vision, and its influence.

Here again, recognition is not bad in itself. Human beings need recognition. Workers, volunteers, employees, elected officials, members, and leaders can accomplish important things that deserve to be acknowledged.

But there is a difference between soberly recognizing a real contribution and organizing a costly system of self-celebration.

When an organization uses collective resources to congratulate itself, the discomfort is obvious. Especially when those who benefit most from this recognition are already those who occupy the most visible positions.

The danger is that the institution ends up serving itself.

It is no longer oriented toward its mission. It is oriented toward its image, its prestige, its internal story, its own importance.

The Symbolic Summit

At a larger scale, this logic takes the form of the symbolic summit.

Leaders meet. Flags are lined up. Photos are taken. Speeches are delivered. Declarations are published. Journalists are present. Security is massive. Protocol dominates.

There may be real discussions. Agreements may be reached. Relationships may be built. But a large part of the machine also serves to produce a symbol: to show that leaders are talking, that institutions are cooperating, that something historic is happening.

The problem is that the symbol can be extremely expensive.

And the question remains: what part of what was done truly required this apparatus? What could have been negotiated in writing, by phone, by videoconference, or by smaller teams? What part of the summit is action, and what part is the staging of power?

The symbolic summit may be the most visible form of unproductive institutional agitation: significant resources used to produce presence, representation, and image.

Why It Persists

This phenomenon persists because it suits many people.

Leaders like to be seen. Organizations like to appear dynamic. Participants enjoy stepping outside the ordinary routine. Budgets exist and tend to be spent. Annual events become traditions. Suppliers, hotels, consultants, speakers, and organizers all have an interest in keeping the machine running.

And above all, no one wants to be the person who says: “Does all this really serve any purpose?”

The critique is socially difficult. Whoever questions a gala seems to despise recognition. Whoever questions a training session seems to despise learning. Whoever questions a meeting seems to reject collaboration. Whoever questions a conference seems not to understand the importance of networking.

This is how partial usefulness protects disproportion.

Yes, there is a benefit. But does that benefit really justify everything being mobilized?

This is the question institutions often avoid.

The Real Measure: Full Cost

Most of the time, the real cost is invisible.

We see the invoice for the room, the caterer, or the equipment. But we do not always calculate person-hours, transportation, preparation, fatigue, delays, loss of focus, postponed work, preparatory meetings, minutes, and follow-ups.

A one-day training session with thirty people is not just a training session. It is thirty workdays, plus organization, plus travel, plus direct costs.

A two-day conference is not just an event. It is a massive suspension of productive time, accompanied by significant logistical expenses.

A two-hour meeting may seem free. It never is.

Institutional time is a collective resource.

Wasting it is a management failure. Wasting it to produce prestige or self-congratulation is a moral failure.

The Central Criterion: Disproportion

The right question is not: “Does this activity serve any purpose?”

Almost everything serves some purpose.

A gala recognizes. A conference connects people. A meeting allows exchange. A training session transmits knowledge. A field outing makes things concrete. A summit sends a political signal.

The real question is:

Does what this activity produces justify what it consumes?

And more specifically:

  • What is the total cost, including person-hours?
  • Who truly benefits from the activity?
  • What concrete result will be produced?
  • Would a more modest option have produced almost the same benefit?
  • Would the expense still be defensible if it were clearly presented to the public, dues-paying members, or stakeholders?
  • Would the activity still be organized if it had to be justified from scratch?

These questions change everything.

They shift the debate from intention to proportion.

Social Value Is Not a Blank Check

The main objection must be acknowledged: human encounters have value.

That is true.

Socializing, cohesion, trust, informal exchanges, recognition, and belonging can have real effects. An organization is not only a production machine. It is also made of human relationships.

But this truth must not become a blank check.

Social value does not automatically justify the hotel, the gala, the travel, the stage, the lighting, the long meetings, the expensive meals, the oversized delegations, or the poorly designed training sessions.

People can be recognized soberly. Training can be effective. Socializing can happen without excess. People can meet when necessary. Cohesion can be built without turning every simple need into a heavy event.

The point is not to eliminate all meetings.

The point is to restore proportion.

Toward Institutional Sobriety

What should be defended is not a cold, distant, entirely virtual institution without recognition or human connection.

What should be defended is institutional sobriety.

Institutional sobriety means choosing the simplest, most direct, and most proportionate means to achieve a real objective.

That may mean:

  • replacing a meeting with a clear document;
  • limiting a meeting to the people truly concerned;
  • calculating person-hours before convening people;
  • replacing passive training with a short video and a corrected exercise;
  • reserving physical presence for situations where it truly adds something;
  • reducing delegations;
  • publishing the full cost of events;
  • replacing costly galas with sober forms of recognition;
  • clearly separating training, socializing, and celebration;
  • requiring a post-event assessment: what changed?
  • abolishing recurring activities that can no longer be justified.

Institutional sobriety does not mean doing nothing.

It means stopping the confusion between doing a lot and doing something useful.

A Matter of Responsibility

Unproductive institutional agitation is not merely a bad administrative habit. It is a way for institutions to convert collective resources into presence, prestige, comfort, discourse, socializing, and internal gratification.

The scandal is not that a meeting, training session, conference, or gala has some usefulness.

The scandal is that this limited usefulness is used to justify arrangements with no proportion to the real benefits.

A responsible institution should not only ask: “Do we have the budget?”

It should ask:

Are we morally justified in using these resources this way?

Because collective resources do not exist to feed the image of institutions, to provide leaders with opportunities for prestige, or to turn internal socializing into public expense.

They exist to serve a mission.

And every time an institution forgets that, it is not merely inefficient.

It abuses the trust that was granted to it.
