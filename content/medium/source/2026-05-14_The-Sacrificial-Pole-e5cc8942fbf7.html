The Sacrificial Pole

The Christian idea that Jesus takes away the sin of the world once made no sense to me. I took it to be one more strange metaphor, another far-fetched invention meant to heap as much significance and glory onto Jesus as possible. It sounded grand, but not intelligible. I could hear the religious force of the phrase, but I could not see its logic.

What changed my mind was not a sermon, but a physical process.

If you want to remove rust from a piece of metal by electrolysis, you can prepare a bath with water and either sodium carbonate or sodium bicarbonate. Then you connect a power supply: the negative lead goes to the piece you want to save, which becomes the cathode, and the positive lead goes to a sacrificial piece of metal, which becomes the anode. The rusted object is the one you hope to restore. The sacrificial metal is the one that will take the burden of the process. Current passes through the bath, the rust loosens from the piece you want to save, and the sacrificial piece is gradually consumed.

Strictly speaking, the rust does not simply travel like dust from one object to another. Chemistry is more precise than that. But at the level of visible intuition, it really does seem as though the corruption leaves the piece one wants to preserve and goes to attack the sacrificial metal instead. One piece is gradually restored. The other assumes the cost.

That image stayed with me.

I am not saying that chemistry proves theology. I am saying that it gave me a structure for thinking. It offered a concrete analogy for an idea that had always seemed vague and inflated. If sin is understood only as moral fault, then the claim that Jesus takes away the sin of the world sounds arbitrary. How can the guilt of one person be removed by another? It feels like religious accounting, or perhaps like poetic exaggeration. But if sin is understood as corruption, then something begins to come into focus.

Rust is not merely a stain. It is a deterioration. It attaches itself to metal, weakens it, deforms it, eats away at its integrity. Sin, understood spiritually, can be seen in a similar way. It is not only a wrong action that can be listed and judged. It is also something that settles into a person and alters him. Fear becomes hardness. Humiliation becomes cruelty. Lies become a way of seeing. Resentment becomes identity. What begins as action becomes condition. What begins as choice becomes corrosion.

That is why the image of rust is so strong. It captures the difference between legal guilt and inner deformation. A law can tell you that something is wrong. But corrosion describes what wrongness does when it accumulates in a person, or in a society. It does not merely condemn; it degrades. It changes texture. It spreads. It reproduces itself.

Once I began to think of sin this way, the figure of Christ changed for me.

If God wanted to send someone into a corrupted world, not merely to lecture it or condemn it from a distance, but to confront its corruption from within, then that person would need to be extraordinarily difficult to corrupt. He would have to be capable of entering hatred without becoming hateful, entering violence without becoming violent, entering injustice without becoming unjust. He would have to stand in the middle of the human bath and absorb what destroys others without letting it become his own interior law.

That, to me, is where Jesus becomes intelligible in a new way.

Today Jesus is one of the most recognized figures in history. But that can make us forget what he would have looked like in his own time: controversial, unsettling, strange, socially dangerous. He was not simply embraced as an obvious moral hero. He disturbed people. He scandalized people. His language could sound bizarre. Talk of eating his flesh and drinking his blood could easily be heard as madness or blasphemy. It is not difficult to imagine how such words could become fuel for hostile rumor and anti-Christian propaganda. The early Christian message was not born as a smooth and universally attractive message. It was offensive, disruptive, and hard to classify.

That matters, because it means Jesus did not move through the world under the protection of easy admiration. He stood inside misunderstanding. He stood inside suspicion. He stood inside a world already thick with power, sacrifice, public religion, crowd psychology, and violence. And in that world, sacrifice itself had a visible social dimension. The poor offered what they could. The rich could offer much more. Sacrifice could become spectacle. Jesus does not answer that world by offering the grandest object. He offers himself.

Not in the sense of theatrical self-destruction, but in the sense of fidelity. He sees where his path is leading. He knows he is provoking forces larger than himself. He could have fled. He could have softened his message. He could have preserved his life. Instead, he remains. He stays faithful to what he is saying and to the way he is saying it, even when that fidelity makes his death increasingly foreseeable.

This is why the cross matters so much.

The cross is not only an execution. It is also a test. Many people speak of love while life is still kind to them. Many praise forgiveness while they have not yet been broken. Many condemn violence until violence falls on them personally. The decisive question is what a person becomes under concentrated injustice. Does he mirror it? Does he pass it on? Does he let evil write his response for him?

In the Christian imagination, Jesus does not.

Fear, mockery, betrayal, cowardice, cruelty, public humiliation, and state violence all converge on him. Yet he does not become a transmitter of the same thing. He receives the blow, but he does not return the same blow as the law of his being. He absorbs violence without making violence his truth. In that sense, the corruption of the world meets in him something it cannot successfully corrupt.

This is where the electrolysis image becomes, for me, more than a clever comparison.

In the bath, the metal you want to save is attached as the cathode. The sacrificial metal is attached as the anode. The bath carries the current. The cathode is the wounded object. The anode bears the cost. Corrosion leaves the object being restored, while the sacrificial side is consumed. Again, the chemistry is more subtle than the simple image. But as an analogy, it is powerful: the damaged piece does not heal itself alone, and the process of restoration is not free. There is mediation. There is transfer. There is cost. There is sacrifice.

So too with redemption.

Human beings do not always escape corruption by insight alone. We can know the good and still reproduce evil. We can denounce violence and still transmit it. We can want forgiveness and still remain trapped in injury. If that is true, then salvation cannot simply be moral advice. It must be some kind of intervention in the chain itself. It must be the introduction, within history, of a presence strong enough to receive corruption without reproducing it.

Christ, then, can be imagined as the sacrificial pole.

Not because sin is a physical substance that literally travels onto him, and not because the analogy should be forced past its limits. Sin is not rust. Grace is not electricity. Christ does not become sinful the way scrap metal becomes oxidized. But the structure still illuminates something real. The corrupted thing is restored because another pole assumes the burden of the process. The one being saved is not simply told to improve. It is placed in relation to a force and a sacrifice that make restoration possible.

In that light, the phrase “Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world” no longer sounds to me like empty exaltation. It begins to sound like a spiritual description of an actual mechanism of redemption, not a chemical mechanism, but a moral and metaphysical one. The world directs its corruption toward Christ. Hatred, accusation, cruelty, and death converge on him. And instead of relaying them onward, he dissolves them in obedience, in forgiveness, in fidelity, in a love stronger than retaliation.

That is why I no longer hear the Christian claim as nonsense. I hear it as an attempt to say something exact, though in symbolic language: that God sent into the human condition someone capable of taking the full assault of corruption without letting corruption become sovereign in him. Someone who could stand at the center of the process and bear its cost. Someone through whom what is rusted might begin, at last, to be restored.

Salvation, then, is not a legal fiction. It is not merely a declaration that guilt has been erased on paper. It is restoration. It is derusting. It is the work by which corruption loses its hold on what it had deformed.

And Christ, in that work, is not merely a teacher or a symbol. He is the sacrificial pole through which the corrosion of sin encounters, and is finally overcome by, what it cannot corrupt.
